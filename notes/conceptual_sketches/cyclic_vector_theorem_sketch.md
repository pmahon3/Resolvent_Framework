# Cyclic Vector Theorem — Mathematical Sketch

**Date:** 2026-04-04  
**Status:** Working sketch — resolving a gap in the program overview statement

---

## The goal

Paper III claims:

> $\sigma(B_\infty) = \mathcal{B}(X)$ mod $\mu$ if and only if $h$ is a cyclic vector
> for the Koopman operator $U_T$.

This sketch shows the claim as stated is **not correct in general**, identifies
the correct theorem, and proposes the right formulation for Paper III.

---

## Setup

Fix:
- $(X, \mathcal{B}, \mu)$: a standard probability space
- $T : X \to X$: measurable, $\mu$-preserving (assume invertible for clean notation)
- $h \in L^2(X, \mu)$
- $U_T : L^2(X,\mu) \to L^2(X,\mu)$, $(U_T f)(x) = f(Tx)$ — unitary when $T$ invertible
  and measure-preserving
- $\mathcal{O}_h := \sigma(\{h \circ T^n : n \in \mathbb{Z}\})$ — the **observable $\sigma$-algebra**
- $\mathcal{H}_h := \overline{\mathrm{span}}\{U_T^n h : n \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ — the **cyclic subspace**
- "$h$ is cyclic for $U_T$" means $\mathcal{H}_h = L^2(X,\mu)$

---

## The density bridge lemma (correct statement)

**Lemma (density bridge).** The following hold:

(i) $\mathcal{H}_h \subseteq L^2(X, \mathcal{O}_h, \mu)$ always.

(ii) $L^2(X, \mathcal{O}_h, \mu)$ is the $L^2$-closure of the **algebra** generated by
    $\{h \circ T^n : n \in \mathbb{Z}\}$, not merely its linear span.

(iii) $L^2(X, \mathcal{O}_h, \mu) = L^2(X, \mathcal{B}, \mu)$ if and only if
     $\mathcal{O}_h = \mathcal{B}$ mod $\mu$.

**Proof sketch.**

(i) Each $h \circ T^n$ is $\mathcal{O}_h$-measurable by definition, so every element of
$\mathrm{span}\{h \circ T^n\}$ is $\mathcal{O}_h$-measurable. $L^2(X, \mathcal{O}_h, \mu)$
is a closed subspace, so $\mathcal{H}_h \subseteq L^2(X, \mathcal{O}_h, \mu)$.

(ii) By the monotone class theorem (functional form): a vector subspace $V$ of $L^\infty$
closed under bounded pointwise limits and containing the constant functions is all of
$L^\infty(\mathcal{O}_h)$ iff it contains all $\mathcal{O}_h$-measurable indicators.
The algebra $\mathcal{A}_h$ generated by $\{h \circ T^n\}$ is dense in $L^2(\mathcal{O}_h)$
by approximating indicators of $\mathcal{O}_h$-sets by polynomials in finitely many
$h \circ T^n$'s (Stone–Weierstrass on the level of measurable functions, or equivalently
the $L^2$ monotone class theorem).

(iii) Immediate: $L^2(X, \mathcal{O}_h, \mu) = L^2(X,\mu)$ iff every $\mathcal{B}$-measurable
$L^2$ function is $\mathcal{O}_h$-measurable mod $\mu$ iff $\mathcal{B} = \mathcal{O}_h$
mod $\mu$.

**The gap:** $\mathcal{H}_h \subsetneq$ closure of $\mathcal{A}_h$ in general. The cyclic
subspace is a linear span; the closure of the algebra is larger. They coincide only under
special conditions on $h$ (see below).

## Counterexample: cyclic ≠ full σ-algebra generation

Take $X = [0,1]$, $\mu = $ Lebesgue, $Tx = 2x \mod 1$ (doubling map), $h(x) = x$.

- The orbit $\{h \circ T^n\}(x) = \{2^n x \mod 1\}$.
- The algebra generated by $\{2^n x \mod 1 : n \geq 0\}$ separates points of $[0,1]$
  a.e., so $\mathcal{O}_h = \mathcal{B}([0,1])$.
- But $\mathrm{span}\{2^n x \mod 1\}$ is a proper subspace of $L^2$ — it does not contain
  $x^2$. So $\mathcal{H}_h \neq L^2$.

Conclusion: **$\mathcal{O}_h = \mathcal{B}$ does NOT imply $h$ cyclic.**

---

## Counterexample: cyclic ≠ full σ-algebra generation (other direction)

Take $X = [0,1]$, $\mu = $ Lebesgue, $T$ = irrational rotation by $\alpha$, $h(x) = e^{2\pi i x}$.

- $\{h \circ T^n\}(x) = \{e^{2\pi i(x + n\alpha)}\}$. Their linear span is the set of
  trigonometric polynomials, which is dense in $L^2([0,1])$ by Weierstrass. So $h$ IS cyclic.
- And $\mathcal{O}_h = \mathcal{B}([0,1])$ since the orbit separates points.

So here both conditions hold simultaneously — but for structural reasons, not because they
are equivalent.

Take instead $h(x) = e^{4\pi i x}$ (omitting $n=0$ harmonic). Then the orbit consists of
even-frequency harmonics $\{e^{4\pi i(x+n\alpha)}\}$. These span a proper closed subspace
of $L^2$ (the even harmonics), so $h$ is NOT cyclic. But $\mathcal{O}_h = \mathcal{B}$
because the even harmonics still generate the Borel $\sigma$-algebra. So: **$h$ cyclic does
NOT imply $\mathcal{O}_h = \mathcal{B}$** ... wait, let me reconsider.

Actually: $e^{4\pi i x}$ on its own generates the $\sigma$-algebra via its orbit because
$T^n(e^{4\pi i x}) = e^{4\pi i(x + n\alpha)}$ and these collectively still separate points.
So $\mathcal{O}_h = \mathcal{B}$ in this case too. The orbit separates points as long as the
level sets $\{x : h(x) = c\}$ are small enough.

Let me try: $h = \mathbf{1}_{[0,1/2]}$ (indicator function). Then $h \circ T^n =
\mathbf{1}_{T^{-n}([0,1/2])}$. The span of $\{h \circ T^n\}$ consists of functions constant
on atoms of the join partition $\bigvee_{n \in S} T^{-n}\mathcal{P}$ for finite $S$ — i.e.,
step functions adapted to the dyadic filtration. This span is dense in $L^2$ for the doubling
map (the dyadic intervals generate all of $\mathcal{B}$). So $h$ IS cyclic here... is it?

$\mathrm{span}\{\mathbf{1}_{T^{-n}([0,1/2])} : n \in \mathbb{Z}\}$: these are indicators of
specific sets, and their linear combinations are step functions on the refinement of these
sets. For the doubling map, $T^{-n}([0,1/2])$ at level $n$ gives the dyadic intervals of
length $2^{-n}$. Their linear span includes all dyadic step functions, which are dense in
$L^2$. So yes, $h = \mathbf{1}_{[0,1/2]}$ is cyclic for the doubling map.

So finding a cyclic $h$ where $\mathcal{O}_h \neq \mathcal{B}$ seems hard — indeed I believe
**$h$ cyclic implies $\mathcal{O}_h = \mathcal{B}$ mod $\mu$** is TRUE.

**Revised picture:**

- $\mathcal{O}_h = \mathcal{B}$ mod $\mu$ iff (algebra density) iff closure of $\mathcal{A}_h = L^2$
- $h$ cyclic iff $\mathcal{H}_h = L^2$
- $\mathcal{H}_h \subseteq$ closure of $\mathcal{A}_h$ always
- So: **$h$ cyclic $\Rightarrow \mathcal{O}_h = \mathcal{B}$** (cyclic is stronger)
- **$\mathcal{O}_h = \mathcal{B}$ does NOT imply $h$ cyclic** (doubling map counterexample)

## The correct theorem: two separate results

### Theorem A (algebra generation = σ-algebra generation)

**(The reconstruction theorem for Paper III)**

Let $(X, \mathcal{B}, \mu)$ be a standard probability space and $T$ a measure-preserving
transformation. For $h \in L^2(X, \mu)$:

$$\mathcal{O}_h = \mathcal{B} \pmod{\mu}
\iff \text{the algebra } \mathcal{A}_h = \mathrm{alg}\{h \circ T^n : n \in \mathbb{Z}\}
\text{ is dense in } L^2(X, \mu).$$

**Proof.**

$(\Rightarrow)$: If $\mathcal{O}_h = \mathcal{B}$ mod $\mu$, then
$L^2(X, \mathcal{O}_h, \mu) = L^2(X, \mu)$. By the density bridge lemma (ii),
closure of $\mathcal{A}_h = L^2(X, \mathcal{O}_h, \mu) = L^2(X, \mu)$.

$(\Leftarrow)$: If closure of $\mathcal{A}_h = L^2$, then in particular every $L^2$ indicator
$\mathbf{1}_A$ (for $A \in \mathcal{B}$) is approximable in $L^2$ by elements of $\mathcal{A}_h$.
Since $\mathcal{A}_h \subseteq L^2(X, \mathcal{O}_h, \mu)$ and $L^2(X, \mathcal{O}_h, \mu)$ is
closed, $\mathbf{1}_A \in L^2(X, \mathcal{O}_h, \mu)$, i.e., $A \in \mathcal{O}_h$ mod $\mu$.
So $\mathcal{B} \subseteq \mathcal{O}_h$ mod $\mu$, giving equality.

### Theorem B (cyclic vector implies reconstruction)

For $h \in L^2(X,\mu)$:

$$h \text{ cyclic for } U_T \implies \mathcal{O}_h = \mathcal{B} \pmod{\mu}.$$

**Proof.**

$\mathcal{H}_h \subseteq$ closure of $\mathcal{A}_h$ (since $\mathcal{A}_h$ contains
$\mathrm{span}\{h \circ T^n\}$). If $h$ is cyclic, $\mathcal{H}_h = L^2$, so
closure of $\mathcal{A}_h = L^2$. By Theorem A, $\mathcal{O}_h = \mathcal{B}$. $\square$

### Theorem C (characterization of cyclic vectors)

**This is the harder direction and the genuinely open piece.**

When does $\mathcal{O}_h = \mathcal{B}$ imply $h$ is cyclic? The doubling map counterexample
shows it does not always hold. The question is: what additional condition on $(T, h, \mu)$
makes the reverse implication true?

**Candidate answer:** $h$ is cyclic for $U_T$ iff:
1. $\mathcal{O}_h = \mathcal{B}$ mod $\mu$ (σ-algebra generation), AND
2. $U_T$ has **simple spectrum** — i.e., $L^2(X,\mu)$ is a cyclic $U_T$-module
   (there exists SOME cyclic vector), AND
3. $h$ is not orthogonal to any $U_T$-reducing subspace.

This is essentially the content of the **spectral multiplicity theory** for unitary operators.
For ergodic $T$, the question of whether $L^2$ has a cyclic vector is the question of whether
$T$ has **simple spectrum** in the ergodic-theoretic sense.

**Key insight:** The program overview conflates two things:
- "Reconstruction" = $\mathcal{O}_h = \mathcal{B}$ (observable layer exhausts state space)
- "Cyclic vector" = $h$ generates all of $L^2$ under $U_T$ (spectral condition)

These are related but not equivalent. Paper III should be about **reconstruction** (the
$\sigma$-algebra condition), stated via **Theorem A**. The cyclic vector condition is a
sufficient condition for reconstruction (Theorem B), but the reconstruction theorem itself
is more general.

## Proposed theorem statement for Paper III

**Reconstruction Theorem (revised).**

Let $(X, \mathcal{B}, \mu)$ be a standard probability space, $T$ a measure-preserving
transformation, and $h \in L^2(X, \mu)$. The following are equivalent:

(i) $\sigma(\{h \circ T^n : n \in \mathbb{Z}\}) = \mathcal{B}$ mod $\mu$
    (the delay observations reconstruct the $\sigma$-algebra)

(ii) The algebra $\mathrm{alg}\{h \circ T^n : n \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ is dense in $L^2(X,\mu)$

(iii) The map $\Phi_h : X \to \mathbb{R}^\mathbb{Z}$, $\Phi_h(x) = (h(T^n x))_{n \in \mathbb{Z}}$,
     is a measure-theoretic embedding: $(\Phi_h)_* \mu$ is supported on the image, and
     $\Phi_h^{-1}(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^\mathbb{Z})) = \mathcal{B}(X)$ mod $\mu$

**Corollary (cyclic sufficiency).**

If $h$ is cyclic for $U_T$ (i.e., $\overline{\mathrm{span}}\{U_T^n h : n \in \mathbb{Z}\} = L^2$),
then conditions (i)–(iii) hold.

**Remark (connection to spectral theory).**

Conditions (i)–(iii) characterize when $h$ is a **generating observation** for $(X, T, \mu)$.
This is weaker than cyclicity: a generating observation need not generate all of $L^2$ as a
linear span. Cyclicity implies generation; generation does not imply cyclicity.

The cyclic vector condition is equivalent to $T$ having **simple $L^2$-spectrum** AND $h$
being a spectral generator. For ergodic transformations, simple spectrum is generic in the
Baire category sense (Halmos–Rokhlin density theorem) but not universal.

## The key proof obligation: the density bridge for algebras

The central lemma needed is:

**Lemma (functional monotone class / algebra density).**

Let $(X, \mathcal{B}, \mu)$ be a probability space and $\mathcal{A} \subseteq L^\infty(X,\mu)$
a subalgebra (closed under pointwise products and containing constants). Then:

$$\overline{\mathcal{A}}^{L^2} = L^2(X,\mu) \iff \sigma(\mathcal{A}) = \mathcal{B} \pmod{\mu}.$$

**Proof of $(\Rightarrow)$:** If $\overline{\mathcal{A}}^{L^2} = L^2$, then for any
$A \in \mathcal{B}$, $\mathbf{1}_A \in L^2 = \overline{\mathcal{A}}^{L^2}$. So there exist
$f_n \in \mathcal{A}$ with $f_n \to \mathbf{1}_A$ in $L^2$. Passing to a subsequence,
$f_n \to \mathbf{1}_A$ a.e. Since $\mathcal{A} \subseteq L^\infty(\mathcal{A}^{\sigma})$
where $\mathcal{A}^{\sigma} = \sigma(\mathcal{A})$, and $L^\infty(\mathcal{A}^{\sigma})$ is
closed under a.e. limits of bounded sequences, $\mathbf{1}_A \in L^\infty(\mathcal{A}^{\sigma})$
— but wait, we need to be careful: $f_n$ are not necessarily uniformly bounded in
$L^\infty(\mathcal{A}^{\sigma})$.

*Correction:* The clean argument goes the other way. $\overline{\mathcal{A}}^{L^2} = L^2$
means $(\overline{\mathcal{A}}^{L^2})^\perp = \{0\}$. If $g \perp \mathcal{A}$, then $g \perp f$
for all $f \in \mathcal{A}$, i.e., $\int fg\, d\mu = 0$ for all $f \in \mathcal{A}$. In
particular $\int \mathbf{1}_A g\, d\mu = 0$ for all $A \in \sigma(\mathcal{A})$ (since $\mathbf{1}_A$
is a monotone class limit of algebra elements). So $g$ is orthogonal to all of
$L^2(\sigma(\mathcal{A}))$. If $\sigma(\mathcal{A}) = \mathcal{B}$, this forces $g = 0$.
But this is circular — we need to prove $\sigma(\mathcal{A}) = \mathcal{B}$, not assume it.

*Better argument for $(\Rightarrow)$:* The $\sigma$-algebra $\sigma(\mathcal{A})$ is determined
by the orthogonal complement: $A \in \sigma(\mathcal{A})$ iff $\mathbf{1}_A \in
L^2(\sigma(\mathcal{A})) = (\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{A}})$ where $\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{A}}$ is the
closed subspace generated by $\mathcal{A}$. If $\overline{\mathcal{A}} = L^2$, then
$\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{A}} = L^2$, so $\mathbf{1}_A \in \mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{A}}$ for every
$A \in \mathcal{B}$. But $\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{A}} \subseteq L^2(\sigma(\mathcal{A}))$ (since
$\mathcal{A} \subseteq L^2(\sigma(\mathcal{A}))$ and the latter is closed), so
$\mathbf{1}_A \in L^2(\sigma(\mathcal{A}))$ for all $A \in \mathcal{B}$, meaning
$A \in \sigma(\mathcal{A})$ mod $\mu$. ✓

**Proof of $(\Leftarrow)$:** If $\sigma(\mathcal{A}) = \mathcal{B}$ mod $\mu$, then every
$L^2$ function $f$ is $\mathcal{B}$-measurable = $\sigma(\mathcal{A})$-measurable mod $\mu$.
So $f \in L^2(\sigma(\mathcal{A})) = \overline{\mathcal{A}}^{L^2}$ (the last equality being
the closure-of-algebra = L^2-of-generated-σ-algebra fact, which follows from simple function
approximation: $\mathcal{B}$-simple functions are in $L^2(\sigma(\mathcal{A}))$ and are dense
in $L^2(\mathcal{B})$; every $\sigma(\mathcal{A})$-simple function is approximable in $L^2$ by
elements of $\mathcal{A}$ since $\mathcal{A}$ generates $\sigma(\mathcal{A})$). ✓

**Status of the key step:** The $(\Rightarrow)$ direction works cleanly via the inclusion
$\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{A}} \subseteq L^2(\sigma(\mathcal{A}))$. The $(\Leftarrow)$ direction
requires: $L^2(\sigma(\mathcal{A})) = \overline{\mathcal{A}}^{L^2}$, i.e., every
$\sigma(\mathcal{A})$-measurable $L^2$ function is in the $L^2$-closure of $\mathcal{A}$.

This is the standard fact that if $\mathcal{A}$ is an algebra generating $\sigma(\mathcal{A})$,
then $\mathcal{A}$-simple functions are dense in $L^2(\sigma(\mathcal{A}))$, which in turn is
because the $\mathcal{A}$-simple functions form a dense subalgebra of $L^2(\sigma(\mathcal{A}))$.
This should be in Mathlib via `MeasureTheory.SimpleFunc.dense_range` or similar.

## What this means for the paper programme

**The program overview needs a correction.** Paper III's main theorem should be stated as
**Theorem A** above (equivalence of $\sigma$-algebra reconstruction and algebra density),
not the cyclic vector equivalence. The cyclic vector condition should appear as:
- A **sufficient condition** for reconstruction (Theorem B)
- The correct equivalence only when $T$ has **simple spectrum** (to be noted as a remark)

**The Takens connection:** The classical Takens theorem constructs a diffeomorphic embedding
using $2d+1$ delays. In our setting, the analogous statement is Theorem A(iii): $\Phi_h$ is a
measure-theoretic embedding when $\mathcal{O}_h = \mathcal{B}$. No dimension bound is needed
(we use all delays); the role of the dimension count is replaced by the algebraic density
condition.

For the Takens comparison: on a compact $d$-manifold with a generic $C^2$ flow and a
generic smooth observation function $h$, the algebra $\mathcal{A}_h$ is dense in $L^2$ (since
smooth functions separate points of a manifold and the orbit is dense for minimal flows),
so the reconstruction condition holds. This recovers Takens in the measure-theoretic setting
without differentiability.

## Key questions to resolve before writing Paper III

**Q1.** Should we assume $T$ invertible (for $n \in \mathbb{Z}$) or just measurable
($n \in \mathbb{N}_0$)? The one-sided case ($n \geq 0$) is more natural for causal
observation but the $\sigma$-algebra it generates may be strictly smaller. The
invertible case is cleaner; most ergodic theory is developed there.

**Q2.** Should $h \in L^2$ or $h \in L^\infty$? $L^2$ is the natural domain for cyclic
vectors; $L^\infty$ is needed if we want uniform approximation. For the algebra density
argument, $h \in L^2$ suffices for the $\sigma$-algebra statement; $L^\infty$ is needed
for Stone–Weierstrass arguments. Recommendation: state for $h \in L^\infty$ (most natural
for observation functions) and note it extends to $L^2$ cyclicity separately.

**Q3.** Do we need $\mu$ ergodic? No — the reconstruction theorem holds for any
measure-preserving $T$. Ergodicity gives a cleaner picture (the observable algebra is
either trivial or all of $\mathcal{B}$) but is not needed for the theorem.

**Q4.** What is the connection to Paper I's Stone space? The map $\Phi_h : X \to \mathbb{R}^\mathbb{Z}$
factors as $X \to \mathrm{St}(\mathcal{O}_h) \hookrightarrow \mathbb{R}^\mathbb{Z}$. When
$\mathcal{O}_h = \mathcal{B}$, the first map is a measure-theoretic isomorphism $X \cong
\mathrm{St}(\mathcal{O}_h)$. So the Stone space identification (Program III claim: "Stone
space of observable algebra is the state space") holds as a consequence of the reconstruction
theorem. This needs to be made precise: the Stone space of $\mathcal{O}_h$ as a Boolean algebra
is $\mathrm{St}(\mathcal{O}_h)$, and the identification $X \cong \mathrm{St}(\mathcal{O}_h)$
is measure-theoretic (mod null sets), not topological.

\section*{Summary}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Summary}

This document explains where a specific open problem in the foundations of
quantum logic currently stands. The problem asks: can a \emph{concrete}
system of yes/no questions — one built honestly from subsets of an
underlying sample space, with no exotic algebra — support a probability
assignment that is consistent at every finite stage of questioning, yet
provably cannot be extended to a fully countably-additive probability
measure? Such an assignment would be a genuinely \emph{contextual}
probability: locally coherent, globally impossible to complete. The answer
depends on how rigid the underlying logical structure is required to be.
If the structure is only required to be an \emph{orthomodular poset} — a
mild associativity requirement, weaker than a lattice — then the answer is
\textbf{yes}, and this is a proved theorem of Zermelo--Fraenkel set theory
with choice (ZFC): an explicit example exists. If the structure is required
to be a full \emph{orthomodular lattice} — every pair of questions must
have a well-defined greatest common refinement — then the answer is
\textbf{conjectured to be no}, and this conjecture is currently open. Three
independent finite obstruction results, together with a recently proved
external theorem about a closely related but different mathematical
object, all point the same way: toward the conjecture being true. But none
of them proves it. The exact reason a proof is hard is explained in
Section~5: the obstruction that would be needed is a genuinely infinite
combinatorial phenomenon (living at the level of the first uncountable
ordinal, $\omega_1$), and every finite method that has been tried
provably cannot reach that far. Two precise, still-open mathematical
routes toward closing the gap are stated in Section~6.

\section{The setting, from scratch}
% =======================================================================

We are going to build up, slowly, the mathematical objects the problem is
about. Nothing here requires familiarity with quantum logic; it requires
only comfort with Boolean algebras, measures, and basic order theory. Every
definition gets a one-sentence plain-language gloss before its formal
statement, and a small worked example.

\subsection{Why orthomodular lattices, informally}

Think of a system you can ask yes/no questions about — a physical system,
a database, an experiment. Some questions are \emph{compatible}: you can
ask them together and get a joint answer (``is the coin heads, and is it
Tuesday?''). Classical probability theory assumes \emph{all} questions are
compatible with all others: the collection of possible questions/events
forms a Boolean algebra. Quantum mechanics is the historical motivation
for dropping this assumption. Some pairs of questions (position and
momentum, say) cannot both be asked with a definite joint answer. The
algebraic structure that survives when you keep the order-theoretic
skeleton of ``event $A$ implies event $B$'' and ``the complementary event
of $A$'' but drop the assumption that any two events have a joint
refinement is an \emph{orthomodular lattice}. This document is entirely
about a purely mathematical question concerning such structures; no
physics is assumed or needed.

\subsection{Orthomodular lattices}

\begin{definition}[Orthomodular lattice]
\label{def:oml}
\textbf{Plain gloss:} a lattice of ``events'' with a complementation
operation (``the event did not happen'') that behaves sensibly, except
that we do \emph{not} require it to satisfy the distributive law that
makes an algebra Boolean.

\smallskip
\noindent Formally, an \emph{orthomodular lattice} is a bounded lattice
$L$ (with least element $0$ and greatest element $1$, and meet/join
operations $\wedge,\vee$) equipped with an order-reversing involution
$a \mapsto a^{\perp}$ (the \emph{orthocomplement}) such that:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item $a \vee a^{\perp} = 1$ and $a \wedge a^{\perp} = 0$ for every $a$;
\item $a \le b \implies b^{\perp} \le a^{\perp}$;
\item $a^{\perp\perp} = a$;
\item (\emph{the orthomodular law}) if $a \le b$ then
  $b = a \vee (b \wedge a^{\perp})$.
\end{enumerate}
Two elements $a,b$ are \emph{compatible} (or \emph{orthogonal} if in
addition $a \le b^{\perp}$) according to further standard definitions we
will not need in general form. A Boolean algebra is exactly an
orthomodular lattice in which every two elements are compatible (the
distributive law holds); orthomodular lattices that fail this are the
genuinely non-classical case.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[The smallest non-Boolean example: $MO_2$]
\label{ex:mo2}
Take two disjoint ``two-outcome'' experiments and glue them at their top
and bottom. Concretely: the six-element lattice with least element $0$,
greatest element $1$, and two incomparable pairs of atoms/coatoms
$\{a, a^{\perp}\}$ and $\{b, b^{\perp}\}$, where $a \wedge b = 0$ but $a$
and $b$ are \emph{not} orthogonal in the algebra (there is no third event
that makes $a \vee b$ decompose the way a Boolean union would). This is
the smallest orthomodular lattice that is not a Boolean algebra: it
satisfies the orthomodular law but fails distributivity. It will recur
below as the standard test object for ``does the failure survive being
tiny and finite,'' and the answer for $MO_2$ (and every finite
orthomodular lattice) is always \emph{no} — see Proposition~\ref{prop:finite-obstruction} below.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[A slightly bigger picture: the pentagon]
\label{ex:pentagon}
A second standard small example, often drawn as a five-cycle of atoms
(a \emph{Greechie diagram}: nodes are atoms, edges join atoms that
generate a common Boolean block), is the \emph{pentagon} lattice: five
atoms arranged around a cycle, each pair of \emph{adjacent} atoms
compatible (living in a shared small Boolean piece), but non-adjacent
pairs incompatible. Like $MO_2$, it is a finite, concrete, non-Boolean
orthomodular lattice, and — this will matter later, in Section~7 — it is
a standard test bed for checking whether a proposed structural mechanism
is really doing the work claimed for it, precisely \emph{because} it is
finite and hence automatically tame in the sense defined below.
\end{example}

\subsection{Concrete (set-representable) orthomodular lattices}

Everything in this document concerns \emph{concrete} orthomodular
lattices: ones built honestly out of actual subsets of an actual set,
with no abstract algebra hiding underneath.

\begin{definition}[Concrete $\sigma$-complete orthomodular lattice]
\label{def:concrete}
\textbf{Plain gloss:} a family of subsets of a set $\Omega$ that is closed
under complementation and under taking countable unions of \emph{disjoint}
members, and in which every two members have a genuine set-theoretic
greatest common refinement inside the family.

\smallskip
\noindent Formally, fix a set $\Omega$ (the \emph{sample space}). A
\emph{concrete $\sigma$-class} on $\Omega$ is a family $L \subseteq
\mathcal{P}(\Omega)$ containing $\emptyset$ and $\Omega$, closed under
set-complement, and closed under countable unions of pairwise-disjoint
members of $L$. The order on $L$ is set-inclusion, and the
orthocomplement is set-complement, $E^{\perp} = \Omega \setminus E$. Every
concrete $\sigma$-class is automatically an \emph{orthomodular poset}: a
partially ordered set with orthocomplementation satisfying the
orthomodular law, but where two elements need not have a well-defined
meet inside the family. If, further, every two elements of $L$ possess a
$\subseteq$-greatest common lower bound that is itself a member of $L$
(a genuine lattice meet), we call $L$ a \emph{concrete $\sigma$-complete
orthomodular lattice}. The distinction — poset versus lattice — is exactly
the boundary this entire document is about, and it will be named
\textbf{OMP} (orthomodular poset — meets not required to exist) versus
\textbf{OML} (orthomodular lattice — meets required to exist) from here
on.
\end{definition}

Concreteness matters. An abstract orthomodular lattice can always be
represented, in principle, in some more exotic way; a \emph{concrete} one
is tied down to honest subsets of a set, so its truth-values are honest
membership facts. This is the setting in which the projection lattice of
a Hilbert space, for instance, generically \emph{fails} to be concrete
(Kochen--Specker, discussed in passing below) — but the objects in this
document are always concrete by definition, so that obstruction is simply
not in play here.

\subsection{States: finitely additive and $\sigma$-additive}

\begin{definition}[Two-valued state]
\label{def:state}
\textbf{Plain gloss:} an assignment of ``true''/``false'' to a family of
events that behaves like a genuine yes/no probability: additive over
finite disjoint unions, and (in the stronger version) additive over
\emph{countable} disjoint unions too.

\smallskip
\noindent Let $B$ be a $\perp$-closed subfamily of $L$ (i.e.\ closed under
complementation; $B$ need not be all of $L$). A \emph{two-valued state} on
$B$ is a function $s : B \to \{0,1\}$ with $s(\Omega) = 1$, such that for
every \emph{finite} pairwise-disjoint family $\{A_i\} \subseteq B$ whose
union lies in $B$,
\[
  s\Big(\bigcup_i A_i\Big) = \sum_i s(A_i).
\]
$s$ is \emph{$\sigma$-additive} if the same identity holds for every
\emph{countable} pairwise-disjoint family. A point-evaluation
$\delta_{\omega}(E) := \mathbf{1}[\omega \in E]$ for a fixed $\omega \in
\Omega$ is always a $\sigma$-additive state; such states are called
\emph{Dirac}. Every other state is \emph{non-Dirac}.
\end{definition}

Intuitively: a finitely additive state is a consistent finite-stage
probability assignment. A $\sigma$-additive one survives being pushed to
the countable limit — the assignment to a countable disjoint union really
is the sum of the assignments to the pieces, with no slippage. The gap
between these two notions is exactly where the whole problem lives.

\subsection{Blocks (maximal Boolean subalgebras)}

\begin{definition}[Block]
\label{def:block}
\textbf{Plain gloss:} the largest ``classical'' piece of the structure
containing a given collection of events — the maximal set of mutually
compatible events, which on its own behaves exactly like an ordinary
Boolean algebra.
\smallskip
\noindent A \emph{block} of $L$ is a maximal subfamily $M \subseteq L$
that is pairwise compatible (every two members of $M$ generate a Boolean
subalgebra inside $L$). Every element of $L$ lies in at least one block
(a standard Zorn's-lemma argument), and every block, viewed on its own,
\emph{is} an honest Boolean $\sigma$-algebra of subsets of $\Omega$: within
a single block there is no obstruction at all, and ordinary classical
probability theory applies without qualification.
\end{definition}

This will be the single most important structural fact used below:
\emph{all} non-classicality lives strictly \emph{between} blocks, never
inside one. A countable disjoint family is always tested for
$\sigma$-additivity inside a single block (Theorem~\ref{thm:blocks-boolean}
below makes this precise), so the failure of $\sigma$-additivity we are
chasing is a failure of \emph{assembling} compatible pieces across blocks,
not a failure inside any one block.

\subsection{Essential contextual states: the object we are looking for}

\begin{definition}[Essential contextual state]
\label{def:essential}
\textbf{Plain gloss:} a probability assignment that is completely
reasonable if you only ever ask finitely many questions at a time, but
which no amount of countably-additive bookkeeping can ever complete —
not because of some pathological infinite question, but because the
finite data itself is already incompatible with every possible
$\sigma$-additive completion.

\smallskip
\noindent Fix a concrete $\sigma$-class $L$ on $\Omega$, a finite
$\perp$-closed sub-poset $B \subseteq L$, and a two-valued state $s_0$ on
$B$. Say $s_0$ is \emph{finitely coherent on $L$} if $s_0$ extends to
\emph{some} finitely additive two-valued state defined on all of $L$
(i.e.\ $s_0$ is not locally self-contradictory; it is a legitimate finite
fragment of \emph{some} global finitely additive assignment). Say $s_0$ is
a \emph{$\sigma$-essential contextual state} if $s_0$ is finitely coherent
on $L$, but no $\sigma$-additive two-valued state on all of $L$ extends
it.
\end{definition}

The finite-coherence clause is not decorative. Without it, the definition
would be trivially satisfiable by a self-contradictory finite fragment
that no state of \emph{any} kind extends — an uninteresting failure with
nothing to do with the countable-additivity gap. Section~3 makes this
precise (Proposition~\ref{prop:amendment}); for now, hold onto the
plain-language version: an essential contextual witness is a piece of
probability data that is honestly extendable to \emph{some} weak
(finitely additive) global assignment, yet is provably incompatible with
\emph{every} strong (countably additive) one.

\begin{definition}[Tame]
\label{def:tame}
We say a concrete $\sigma$-class $L$ is \emph{tame} (or satisfies
\emph{finite-trace $\sigma$-liftability}, written $\Phi(L)$) if
\emph{every} finitely coherent finite pattern on $L$ extends to some
$\sigma$-additive two-valued state on $L$ — i.e.\ $L$ admits \emph{no}
$\sigma$-essential contextual state at all. Tameness is the negation of
``carries a witness'': $L$ is tame exactly when the search of this
document, run on $L$, comes back empty.
\end{definition}

\section{The central question, stated}
% =======================================================================

We can now state the problem precisely.

\begin{conjecture}[The central conjecture]
\label{conj:main}
Every concrete $\sigma$-complete orthomodular \emph{lattice} (every two
elements have a genuine meet in the structure) is tame: it admits no
$\sigma$-essential contextual state.
\end{conjecture}

This is the headline question. It is currently \textbf{open}. What is
known, precisely, is the following dichotomy, which this whole document
exists to explain:

\begin{itemize}
\item If $L$ is only required to be a concrete $\sigma$-complete
  orthomodular \emph{poset} (Definition~\ref{def:concrete}, without the
  lattice/meet requirement), then Conjecture~\ref{conj:main}'s analogue is
  \textbf{false}: an explicit witness is proved to exist, in plain ZFC,
  with no extra set-theoretic hypotheses (Theorem~\ref{thm:main} below).
\item Conjecture~\ref{conj:main} itself, for genuine \emph{lattices},
  remains open. The gap between these two facts — poset versus lattice —
  is the entire content of what remains to be understood, and it is
  discussed at length in Sections~4--6.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{A second, equivalent, face of the conjecture}

The conjecture has one genuinely useful reformulation that this programme
has proved equivalent to it (not merely suggestively related to it), and
we record it now because later sections work with whichever face is more
convenient.

\begin{theorem}[Cluster reformulation of tameness]
\label{thm:phi-cluster}
Fix a concrete $\sigma$-class $L$. Call a finite family $\mathcal{C}
\subseteq L$ an \emph{f.a.-coherent cluster} if some finitely additive
two-valued state on $L$ assigns value $1$ to every member of $\mathcal{C}$.
Then the following are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item (\emph{Tameness, block form}) $L$ is tame in the sense of
  Definition~\ref{def:tame}: every finite pattern on a block $B$ that
  agrees with some finitely additive state on $L$ extends to a
  $\sigma$-additive state on $L$.
\item (\emph{Tameness, cluster form}) Every f.a.-coherent cluster
  $\mathcal{C}$ is assigned the value $1$, throughout, by some single
  $\sigma$-additive two-valued state on $L$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\emph{Proof sketch.} Both directions are elementary manipulations that
use no special structure of $L$ (in particular, no latticehood): given a
block-level pattern, close it under complementation and read off its
value-$1$ part as a cluster; conversely, given a cluster, extend it to a
full pattern on the $\perp$-closure of the cluster by declaring the
complements false, and check the state constructed for the cluster form
already satisfies every complement constraint automatically. Full formal
detail is routine bookkeeping and has been machine-verified in both
directions. $\hfill\square$

\emph{Plain gloss.} These are two ways of asking the same question. The
block form says: ``does every consistent finite yes/no pattern, read off
one classical piece at a time, extend to a genuine global probability
measure?'' The cluster form says the same thing more economically: ``does
every finite set of events that some finitely-additive assignment can
jointly mark `true' get simultaneously marked `true' by some
\emph{countably}-additive assignment?'' We use whichever phrasing is
lighter in context; they never disagree.

We do \emph{not} present a third independent face here. A superficially
tempting third reformulation — splitting the negation of tameness into
``no point-evaluation realizes the pattern'' and ``no other
$\sigma$-additive state realizes it either'' — is a genuine and useful
fact, but it is a logical \emph{tautology} (every $\sigma$-additive state
is either a point-evaluation or it isn't) rather than an independent
reformulation of the conjecture; we develop it in Section~3 below
(Theorem~\ref{thm:localization}), where its actual mathematical content
(one of the two halves is always free, so the entire difficulty is
concentrated in the other) belongs.

\section{What is proved: the solid ground}
% =======================================================================

We now build up, as a sequence of labelled theorems, everything that is
actually established. Each is followed by one plain sentence on what it
buys strategically.

\subsection{All non-classicality lives between blocks}

\begin{theorem}[Blocks are Boolean $\sigma$-fields]
\label{thm:blocks-boolean}
Every block $M$ of a concrete $\sigma$-class $L$ is, restricted to
itself, an honest Boolean $\sigma$-algebra of subsets of $\Omega$: it is
closed under complement and under countable unions of pairwise-disjoint
members, and any countable disjoint family entirely contained in $M$
has its (automatically well-defined) union already inside $M$.
Consequently, testing whether a state is $\sigma$-additive on a countable
disjoint family reduces to testing it on the single block containing that
family.
\end{theorem}

\emph{Proof sketch.} A block is by definition a maximal pairwise-compatible
subfamily; pairwise compatibility together with maximality forces closure
under the Boolean operations restricted to $M$, by a standard
Zorn's-lemma/maximality argument common to orthomodular lattice theory. $\hfill\square$

\emph{What this buys.} It licenses the entire strategy of the programme:
because $\sigma$-additivity only ever has to be checked one block at a
time, the search for an essential contextual state reduces to a question
about how compatible finite patterns on \emph{different} blocks can (or
cannot) be simultaneously realized by a single global $\sigma$-additive
state. All of the difficulty is an assembly problem, never a
within-block problem.

\subsection{Why the finite-coherence clause is load-bearing}

\begin{proposition}[Boolean baseline]
\label{prop:amendment}
If $L$ is (all of) a Boolean $\sigma$-algebra of subsets of $\Omega$
(i.e.\ $L$ has only one block, itself), and $s_0$ is finitely coherent on
$L$ in the sense of Definition~\ref{def:essential}, then $s_0$ extends to
a Dirac (hence automatically $\sigma$-additive) state on $L$. Consequently
$L$ carries no $\sigma$-essential contextual state at all: tameness on a
Boolean carrier is not merely conjectured but immediate from the
definitions.
\end{proposition}

\emph{Proof sketch.} Let $\mu \supseteq s_0$ be any global finitely
additive two-valued state witnessing $s_0$'s coherence, and let $B_0$ be
the finite Boolean subalgebra generated by $B$. The atoms of $B_0$
partition $\Omega$ into finitely many pieces; finite additivity of $\mu$
over this partition forces exactly one atom $a$ to carry $\mu$-value $1$,
and $a$ is nonempty. Any point $\omega \in a$ then gives a Dirac state
$\delta_\omega$ agreeing with $\mu$ (hence with $s_0$) on all of $B_0$,
since every member of $B_0$ either contains $a$ or is disjoint from it.
$\hfill\square$

\emph{Why the finite-coherence clause matters.} Without it, one could
manufacture a spurious ``witness'' out of nothing: simply write down a
finite pattern that is locally inconsistent even at the finite,
finitely-additive level (no global finitely additive assignment agrees
with it at all). Such a pattern trivially fails to extend
$\sigma$-additively — but for a vacuous reason that has nothing to do
with the countable-additivity gap this document is about. Requiring
finite coherence rules this out: it forces the pattern to be a genuine
fragment of \emph{some} legitimate global finitely-additive assignment,
so that its failure to extend all the way to a $\sigma$-additive one is
a meaningful, non-trivial fact. Proposition~\ref{prop:amendment} shows
this is not merely a technical nicety: on the most classical carrier of
all (Boolean), finite coherence by itself already forces the strongest
possible outcome, a Dirac extension. Any genuine witness must therefore
live on a carrier where finite coherence does \emph{not} force this —
i.e.\ a non-Boolean one, as the theorem of Section~4 in fact does.

\subsection{Countably generated blocks force triviality}

\begin{theorem}[Countably generated boundary extension]
\label{thm:ctbly-gen}
Let $A$ be a countably generated Boolean $\sigma$-subalgebra of $L$ (i.e.\
generated, as a $\sigma$-algebra, by countably many of its members), and
suppose $A$ embeds faithfully, via a $\sigma$-homomorphism, into a
$\sigma$-complete orthomodular lattice $L$ whose $\sigma$-additive
two-valued states order-separate its elements (distinct nonzero elements
are distinguished by some $\sigma$-state). Then every $\sigma$-additive
two-valued state on $A$ extends to one on $L$.
\end{theorem}

\emph{Proof sketch.} Fix generators $(a_n)_{n<\omega}$ of $A$ and a
$\sigma$-state $\mu$ on $A$. For each $n$ let $b_n = a_n$ if $\mu(a_n)=1$
and $b_n = a_n^{\perp}$ otherwise; the finite conjunctions $b_1 \wedge
\cdots \wedge b_n$ all have $\mu$-value $1$, so countable additivity of
$\mu$ forces their countable meet $c_\mu := \bigwedge_n b_n$ to be
nonzero. Order-separation in $L$ then supplies a $\sigma$-state on $L$
that charges $j(c_\mu)$ (the faithful image of $c_\mu$), and this state
agrees with $\mu$ on every generator, hence on all of $A$. $\hfill\square$

\emph{What this buys.} It rules out an entire class of would-be
witnesses at a stroke: if the finite pattern you are trying to make
essential lives inside — or can be faithfully and separatingly embedded
into — a \emph{countably generated} piece of the structure, it will
always extend. Any witness, if one exists on a lattice, cannot be
countably generated in this separating sense; genuine uncountability of
structure is a necessary ingredient, not an incidental feature.

\subsection{The Dirac/non-Dirac partition (a structural tautology, with
real content)}

\begin{theorem}[Localization to the non-Dirac core]
\label{thm:localization}
Fix a finite $\perp$-closed $B \subseteq L$ and a two-valued state $s_0$
on $B$. Then $s_0$ extends to \emph{no} $\sigma$-additive two-valued
state on $L$ if and only if both of the following hold:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item no Dirac (point-evaluation) state extends $s_0$: equivalently,
  $\bigcap \{A \in B : s_0(A) = 1\} = \emptyset$;
\item no \emph{non-Dirac} $\sigma$-additive two-valued state extends
  $s_0$ either.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\emph{Proof.} Every $\sigma$-additive two-valued state is, by
Definition~\ref{def:state}, either Dirac or non-Dirac, and these two
possibilities are mutually exclusive and jointly exhaustive; ``no
$\sigma$-state extends $s_0$'' is therefore logically equivalent to ``no
Dirac state extends $s_0$, and no non-Dirac one does either.'' The
restatement of clause (i) as an intersection condition is immediate: a
point $\omega$ satisfies $\delta_\omega \restriction B = s_0$ exactly
when $\omega$ lies in every $s_0$-true member of $B$ (the $s_0$-false
constraints are automatic once the true ones are met, by
$\perp$-closure of $B$). $\hfill\square$

\emph{Why this is a tautology, and why it is still useful.} The
equivalence itself is definitionally forced — it says nothing more than
``a state is Dirac or it isn't'' — and so it is \emph{not}, on its own,
an independent reformulation of tameness in the sense of
Theorem~\ref{thm:phi-cluster}. Its value is entirely strategic: clause
(i) above (does some point realize the pattern) is a purely finite,
completely checkable condition, and — as Section~4's construction shows
by explicit example — it can always be arranged to hold or fail as
desired, essentially for free, independently of clause (ii). This means
the \emph{entire} difficulty of finding or ruling out a witness collapses
onto clause (ii): the question of whether some \emph{non-Dirac}
$\sigma$-additive state realizes the pattern. Every substantive result in
the rest of this section, and the construction of Section~4, is
usefully read as being \emph{about} clause (ii), with clause (i) treated
as settled machinery.

\subsection{The necessary condition on any witness, with its exact
choice-strength}

This is the sharpest structural fact proved to date about what a witness
\emph{must} look like, and it comes with an unusually precise calibration
of how much of the axiom of choice it actually requires — worth spelling
out carefully, because the calibration itself is part of the content.

\begin{theorem}[Non-extendability, and the equivalence it sits inside]
\label{thm:nonextend}
Let $s_0$ be a two-valued state on a finite $\perp$-closed $B \subseteq
L$, and let $V := \{A \in B : s_0(A) = 1\}$ (the ``value-$1$ family'' of
$s_0$). The following three statements are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]
\item some point $\omega \in \Omega$ lies in every member of $V$ (i.e.\
  $s_0$ is realized by a Dirac state);
\item $s_0$ extends to a two-valued state on the Boolean $\sigma$-algebra
  generated by $B$;
\item $V$ has the \emph{finite intersection property}: every finite
  subfamily of $V$ has nonempty intersection.
\end{enumerate}
Directions $(1)\Rightarrow(3)$ and $(2)\Rightarrow(3)$ hold in plain ZF,
with no choice at all. The remaining direction, $(3)\Rightarrow(2)$, is
\emph{exactly} an application of the Boolean Prime Ideal theorem (BPI) —
the standard weak form of the axiom of choice equivalent to ``every
proper filter on a Boolean algebra extends to an ultrafilter'' — and no
weaker principle suffices for this step in general.

Consequently: if $s_0$ is a $\sigma$-essential contextual state, then
(since no Dirac state realizes it, by definition of essentiality) $V$
fails the finite intersection property at some \emph{finite} stage: some
finite subfamily of the value-$1$ family already has empty intersection.
This finite-stage failure statement itself is a plain ZF consequence
(via the contrapositive of $(1) \Leftrightarrow (3)$, which needs no
choice) — it is the \emph{abstract three-way equivalence}, not the
concrete failure fact, that is calibrated exactly at BPI.
\end{theorem}

\emph{Proof sketch.} $(1) \Rightarrow (3)$: if some $\omega$ lies in every
member of $V$, every finite subfamily of $V$ meets at $\omega$, trivially.
$(2) \Rightarrow (3)$: an extension to the generated Boolean
$\sigma$-algebra, being finitely additive and two-valued there, forces
finite intersections of value-$1$ sets to remain value-$1$ (hence
nonempty), by the ordinary finite-additivity argument inside a Boolean
algebra. $(3) \Rightarrow (2)$: the finite intersection property makes $V$
a filter base; extend it to an ultrafilter on the generated Boolean
algebra (this step is the Boolean Prime Ideal theorem, precisely) and read
off the corresponding two-valued state. $\hfill\square$

\emph{What this buys, and why the choice-strength matters.} Strategically:
any witness search can restrict attention, without loss, to finite value-1
families that fail to have a common point at some \emph{finite}, checkable
stage — this is a sharp, computationally testable necessary condition, and
it was checked directly against the one known witness (Theorem~\ref{thm:main}
below): its value-$1$ family fails at the smallest possible stage, three.
For the reverse-math-minded reader: the fact that direction
$(3)\Rightarrow(2)$ needs exactly BPI, and not more, tells you precisely
how ``constructive'' this piece of the theory is — the \emph{obstruction}
itself (the finite-stage failure, used as a necessary condition on
witnesses) is choice-free, while the \emph{positive} extension direction
genuinely needs a weak form of choice and nothing stronger. This is a
clean example of a place where the reverse-mathematical strength of a
classical-logic fact has been pinned down exactly rather than left vague.

\subsection{The finite obstruction results}

\begin{proposition}[No finite or countably-generated witness]
\label{prop:finite-obstruction}
No \emph{finite} concrete orthomodular poset (lattice or not) carries a
$\sigma$-essential contextual state: on a finite carrier, every
finitely additive two-valued state is automatically $\sigma$-additive
(there is nothing infinite to fail to converge on), so tameness is
automatic. More specifically, the following assembly obstruction holds:
no concrete $\sigma$-complete orthomodular \emph{lattice} can contain two
distinct ``puncture'' Boolean blocks that (a) each faithfully and
order-separatingly represent the same countable decreasing clopen
neighbourhood basis at a point, and (b) identify that shared boundary
data between the two blocks. Concretely: if $(U_n)_{n<\omega}$ is a
decreasing sequence of clopen sets in a Cantor-space-like block converging
to a single point $i$, and this same countable boundary sequence is glued
identically into a second block converging instead to a different point
$j \ne i$, then the countable meet $\bigwedge_n U_n$ evaluates to $\{i\}$
in the first block's intrinsic order and to (something containing) $\{j\}
\ne \{i\}$ in the second — an outright contradiction, since the meet of a
fixed countable family is an intrinsic feature of the ambient
$\sigma$-complete lattice and cannot depend on which block computes it.
\end{proposition}

\emph{Proof sketch.} The meet of a decreasing countable family in a
$\sigma$-complete lattice is computed once, in the ambient structure, via
complementation and the countable-join closure; it cannot simultaneously
equal two different singleton-supported values as seen from two different
faithful embeddings, since both embeddings realize the \emph{same}
ambient meet. $\hfill\square$

\emph{What this buys.} This rules out the most naive way of trying to
build a witness by gluing together countably many copies of a
Cantor-space-like Boolean piece along a shared boundary: the gluing
itself is already inconsistent with latticehood, independently of
centrality (independently, that is, of whether the shared piece sits in
the ``classical center'' of the structure or not). It is one of the three
obstruction results whose combined weight is discussed in Section~5.

\begin{proposition}[The countably-generated route is fully closed]
\label{prop:ctbly-gen-full}
Combining Theorem~\ref{thm:ctbly-gen} with a standard fact about standard
Borel spaces (the Lusin--Souslin injection theorem: if countably many
countably-generated quotient algebras of a standard Borel space jointly
separate its points, the algebra they generate is already the full Borel
$\sigma$-algebra), one obtains: no \emph{countably generated}, faithfully
and separatingly embedded boundary structure can support the missing
$\sigma$-state either — the same triviality forced by
Theorem~\ref{thm:ctbly-gen} recurs however the countably-generated pieces
are combined, provided they remain countably many.
\end{proposition}

\emph{What this buys.} It closes off, completely, the entire family of
constructions built from only countably much combinatorial data. Any
surviving construction has to be genuinely, unavoidably
uncountably-generated — this sharpens ``the difficulty is infinitary''
from a vague slogan into a theorem.

\section{The one existing witness: a proved ZFC theorem}
% =======================================================================

We now state, precisely, the one witness that has actually been
constructed and proved. It settles the orthomodular-\emph{poset} form of
the question completely; it is the reason the poset/lattice distinction
is the entire remaining content.

\begin{theorem}[A $\sigma$-essential contextual state exists, in ZFC]
\label{thm:main}
There is a concrete $\sigma$-complete orthomodular \emph{poset} $L$ (built
honestly from subsets of a set $\Omega$ of cardinality $\aleph_1$, the
first uncountable cardinal) carrying a finite pattern $s_0$ that is a
$\sigma$-essential contextual state, in the sense of
Definition~\ref{def:essential}. The proof uses only the ordinary axioms
of ZFC (in particular, the axiom of choice in its usual strength — the
proof cites the ultrafilter/prime-ideal principle plus the standard fact
that every filter extends to an ultrafilter, both consequences of full
choice as normally assumed) — no large cardinal or independence
hypothesis of any kind is invoked or needed.
\end{theorem}

\emph{Proof sketch, at the conceptual level (full detail is a substantial
independent development, sketched here in outline).} The construction has
three moving parts, deliberately kept from interacting except through
countable ``noise'':

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item \textbf{An Ulam matrix.} On a set $M$ of size $\aleph_1$ with a
  well-ordering whose every initial segment is countable, one builds
  (using choice, one injection per ordinal) a doubly-indexed family of
  subsets $\{C_{\alpha,n} : \alpha < \omega_1,\ n < \omega\}$ such that:
  each ``row'' $\{C_{\alpha,n}\}_{n<\omega}$ is pairwise disjoint and
  covers all of $M$ except a countable set; and each ``column''
  $\{C_{\alpha,n}\}_{\alpha<\omega_1}$ (fixed $n$) is pairwise disjoint
  across different rows. This is a classical device going back to Ulam's
  work on measurable cardinals, repurposed here for a different end.

\item \textbf{A rigidity theorem.} Enlarge the carrier to $\Omega = M
  \times \{1,2,3,4\}$ (four ``fibers'' over each point of $M$), and let
  $L$ be generated by all singletons, by the ``cells''
  $D_{\alpha,n} := C_{\alpha,n} \times \{1,2,3,4\}$, and by three further
  distinguished sets $A, B, C$ described in part (c). One proves: every
  $\sigma$-additive two-valued state on $L$ must be a Dirac (point)
  state. The argument is a clean pigeonhole: if a state kills every
  singleton (hence, by $\sigma$-additivity, every countable set), then in
  each row exactly one cell must carry value $1$; but only countably many
  cells exist per row, while there are uncountably many rows, so two
  different rows are forced (by the pigeonhole principle applied to an
  uncountable-into-countable map) to select the \emph{same} column
  index; the corresponding two cells are disjoint by the Ulam matrix's
  column-disjointness property, so additivity would force their union to
  have value $2$ — impossible for a $\{0,1\}$-valued state. Hence no
  non-Dirac $\sigma$-state can exist at all: rigidity.

\item \textbf{A finite pattern realized by an ultrafilter's ``vote,'' but
  by no point.} The three sets $A = M\times\{1,2\}$, $B = M\times\{1,3\}$,
  $C = M\times\{2,3\}$ have every pairwise intersection nonempty (e.g.\
  $A \cap B = M \times \{1\}$) but empty triple intersection ($A \cap B
  \cap C = \emptyset$) — the value-$1$ family of the intended pattern
  fails the finite intersection property at the smallest possible stage,
  three, exactly matching Theorem~\ref{thm:nonextend}'s necessary
  condition. One then fixes, using choice, a non-principal ultrafilter on
  $M$ and uses it to define a finitely additive two-valued state $m$ on
  all of $L$ that assigns value $1$ to each of $A,B,C$ simultaneously (a
  short parity computation shows the three assignments are jointly
  consistent even though no single point can witness all three at once).
  This $m$ certifies finite coherence: the pattern $s_0 := m$ restricted
  to the finite piece generated by $A,B,C$ is a legitimate two-valued
  state on a finite sub-poset, extendable (by $m$ itself) to a full
  finitely additive state on all of $L$.
\end{enumerate}

Putting the pieces together: $s_0$ is finitely coherent (by the state
$m$), but by rigidity (part (b)) the \emph{only} candidates for a
$\sigma$-additive extension are Dirac states, and no point $\omega \in
\Omega$ lies in $A \cap B \cap C = \emptyset$ — so no Dirac state
extends $s_0$ either. Hence $s_0$ is a $\sigma$-essential contextual
state, proving the theorem. $\hfill\square$

\emph{Why the carrier is not a lattice.} A key structural fact about this
witness, worth stating on its own, is that the carrier $L$ constructed
above is provably \emph{not} a lattice.

\begin{proposition}[The witness is an orthomodular poset, not a lattice]
\label{prop:not-lattice}
In the carrier $L$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}, the two sets $A$ and $B$
have \emph{no} greatest lower bound inside $L$: every member of $L$
contained in $A \cap B = M \times \{1\}$ is a \emph{countable} subset of
that set, and among the countable subsets of an uncountable set there is
no largest one (one can always adjoin one more missing point and remain
inside $L$, since $L$ contains every countable set). So the meet
$A \wedge B$ simply fails to exist in $L$: $L$ is a genuine orthomodular
\emph{poset}, not an orthomodular \emph{lattice}.
\end{proposition}

\emph{What this buys, and why it is the crux of the whole document.} It
is precisely this fact — that the one proved witness lives on a
\emph{poset}, not a \emph{lattice} — that keeps Conjecture~\ref{conj:main}
open. The theorem above resolves the poset form of the question
completely and unconditionally; it says nothing, one way or the other,
about the lattice form, because the object it constructs is not a
lattice. Every later section is, in one way or another, about this exact
gap.

\section{The external boundary result: measure extension on operator
algebras}
% =======================================================================

A recent and closely related development sits just outside this
programme's own object of study, in a genuinely different (though
related) mathematical world — the projection lattices of certain
operator algebras — and it is important to state precisely what it does
and does not tell us.

\begin{extthm}[Escolano--Peralta--Villena, 2025]
\label{thm:epv}
Let $J$ be a JBW$^{*}$-algebra (a certain class of complex Jordan Banach
algebras that generalize von Neumann algebras and includes the
self-adjoint operator algebras relevant to quantum mechanics), and let
$P(J)$ denote its lattice of projections. Suppose $J$ has \emph{no direct
summand of type $I_2$} (type $I_2$ is the smallest genuinely
non-distributive building block that can occur in this classification —
essentially, a spin factor, the operator-algebraic analogue of the
lattice $MO_2$ from Example~\ref{ex:mo2}). Then:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item every bounded finitely additive real- or complex-valued measure on
  $P(J)$ extends to a bounded linear functional on $J$;
\item more generally (via Hahn--Banach), every bounded finitely additive
  measure on $P(J)$ valued in an arbitrary Banach space extends to a
  bounded linear operator on $J$.
\end{enumerate}
\emph{Sharpness (the dual result).} If $J$ \emph{does} have a type
$I_2$ summand, this fails: there exists a positive, bounded, finitely
additive measure on $P(J)$ that does \emph{not} extend to a bounded
linear functional. So the no-$I_2$ hypothesis is exactly sharp, not
merely sufficient.
\end{extthm}

\emph{The mechanism, at a conceptual level.} The proof of the positive
direction (i) does not proceed via any infinitary or set-theoretic device.
It establishes that, away from type $I_2$, every bounded finitely
additive measure on the projection lattice is automatically \emph{uniformly
continuous} — nearby projections receive close measure values — and
uniform continuity is then upgraded to full linear extendability by
standard functional-analytic machinery. The uniform-continuity step itself
rests on three purely geometric/algebraic operations available on
projections once type $I_2$ is excluded: \emph{halving} a projection into
two smaller, mutually equivalent orthogonal pieces; \emph{small symmetric
motions} that exchange two nearby projections while moving everything else
only a controlled amount; and \emph{isoclinic interpolation}, inserting a
third projection at a controlled angle to two given ones so as to bridge
between them in a finite number of algebraic steps. None of this uses
anything about uncountable cardinals, ultrafilters, or countable additivity
in the sense of this document's Definition~\ref{def:state} — it is,
in the relevant technical sense, an \emph{elementary} (finite,
constructive) mechanism.

\begin{remark}[What this does and does not tell us for the lattice
problem]
\label{rem:epv-scope}
Theorem~\ref{thm:epv} is genuinely useful, and genuinely limited, in the
following precise ways.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{What it does.} It \emph{narrows the target}: if a
witness for the analogous phenomenon in the JBW$^{*}$-projection-lattice
world exists at all, it must live in the type-$I_2$, ``symmetry-poor''
regime — everywhere else, the theorem forbids it outright. This gives, for
the first time, a precise structural profile for where such a
phenomenon-of-this-general-shape can occur in that adjacent world.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{What it does not do.} Three separate limitations, each
load-bearing:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Different carriers.} The theorem is about \emph{JBW$^{*}$
  projection lattices}. The concrete $\sigma$-complete orthomodular
  lattices and posets that are the subject of this entire document
  (Definition~\ref{def:concrete}) are not, in general, JBW$^{*}$
  projection lattices — they need not carry any compatible norm or Jordan
  algebra structure at all. The theorem simply does not apply to them
  directly, in either direction: it neither forbids nor supplies a
  witness on the concrete carriers of interest here.
\item \emph{The mechanism does not match the obstruction needed here.}
  Even restricted to its own setting, the dual (sharpness) direction of
  Theorem~\ref{thm:epv} — the existence of a non-extendable measure on a
  type-$I_2$ summand — arises from an elementary, finite piece of linear
  algebra (a rank-counting contradiction inside a small spin factor: a
  measure assigning value $\tfrac{1}{2}$ to rank-one projections forces
  an arithmetic clash, $\tfrac{1}{3} \ne \tfrac{1}{2}$, in a specific
  three-dimensional computation). This is categorically \emph{not} the
  kind of obstruction the lattice conjecture (Conjecture~\ref{conj:main})
  needs: that conjecture, if false, would need a genuinely
  \emph{infinitary}, $\omega_1$-scale combinatorial phenomenon (exactly
  the kind built in Theorem~\ref{thm:main}), not a finite linear-algebra
  clash. The two mechanisms live at different scales entirely.
\item \emph{It narrows a location, not a proof.} Even taking the
  ``symmetry-poor, type-$I_2$-rich'' heuristic at face value as a
  suggestion for \emph{where}, in the concrete-lattice world, a witness
  might have to live (were one to exist), it supplies no argument for
  \emph{whether} one exists there. It is a candidate-location filter, not
  a partial proof.
\end{itemize}
\end{remark}

\emph{Summary of this section, in one sentence.} Theorem~\ref{thm:epv} is
a genuine, recent, sharp external result that narrows where an analogous
phenomenon could live in an adjacent mathematical world, but it neither
applies to, nor resolves, nor even meaningfully constrains a proof
strategy for, the concrete-lattice conjecture that is the subject of this
document; it is correctly read as \emph{narrowing a target}, not as
\emph{a step toward a solution}.

\section{Where the difficulty actually is: the honest crux}
% =======================================================================

We now assemble what has been built up into a single, plain-language
account of exactly what makes Conjecture~\ref{conj:main} hard, and why
every finite technique available provably cannot reach it.

If a witness for the \emph{lattice} form of the conjecture exists, it must
satisfy four requirements simultaneously, each individually forced by the
results of Sections 3--4:

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item \textbf{Genuinely uncountably-generated.} By
  Theorem~\ref{thm:ctbly-gen} and Proposition~\ref{prop:ctbly-gen-full},
  no witness can be built from — or faithfully, separatingly embedded
  into — countably much combinatorial data. The one existing (poset-level)
  witness needs a carrier of size $\aleph_1$; nothing smaller is known to
  suffice, and the countably-generated route is entirely closed.

\item \textbf{Living in a ``symmetry-poor'' regime.} Insofar as the
  external boundary result (Theorem~\ref{thm:epv}, understood only as a
  heuristic pointer in the different-but-related operator-algebra world)
  is any guide at all, the relevant phenomena cluster where the local
  algebraic symmetry is minimal — the type-$I_2$-like corner, not a
  richly homogeneous one.

\item \textbf{Carrying a genuinely contextual $\sigma$-additive-seeming
  state.} By Theorem~\ref{thm:nonextend}, the finite pattern in question
  must fail the finite intersection property already at some finite
  stage; by Definition~\ref{def:essential}, it must nonetheless be
  finitely coherent — i.e.\ it is not merely inconsistent nonsense, it is
  a real finite fragment of some finitely-additive global assignment,
  which is what makes its failure to extend $\sigma$-additively
  meaningful rather than vacuous.

\item \textbf{Surviving lattice closure.} This is the hard core, and it
  deserves its own explanation.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Why requirement (iv) is the genuine obstacle}

The one proved witness (Theorem~\ref{thm:main}) achieves (i)--(iii) with
room to spare. What it cannot achieve — and what
Proposition~\ref{prop:not-lattice} shows it provably fails to achieve — is
(iv): its carrier is not a lattice. The two events $A$ and $B$ have no
greatest common refinement in the structure at all. This is not an
accident of one particular construction; it recurs across every
construction this programme has attempted (the ``countable-meet
obstruction'' of Proposition~\ref{prop:finite-obstruction} is a second,
independent instance of the same phenomenon, arising from a completely
different building strategy). In every case examined, the mechanism that
makes the finite pattern genuinely essential (no single point can
simultaneously satisfy every constraint) is the \emph{same} mechanism
that, when one tries to force the structure to have all its meets and
joins after all, either destroys the essential pattern (some meet, once
it is forced to exist, ends up realizing the pattern after all, at a
single point) or is simply unavailable to compute (the required meet
provably does not exist, as in Proposition~\ref{prop:not-lattice}).

Concretely: requiring $L$ to be a lattice means requiring, for
\emph{every} pair of elements, that a specific new element — their meet
— be manufactured and adjoined to the structure if it is not already
present, in a way that is consistent with everything else (associativity,
the orthomodular law, all prior additivity constraints). Doing this for
\emph{countably many} pairs at once, in a coherent, simultaneous way, is
already delicate; doing it for the \emph{uncountably many} pairs that
necessarily arise once the carrier is forced to be uncountable
(requirement (i) above) is a fundamentally different order of
combinatorial difficulty. It is, in the precise technical sense used
throughout this document, a question about $\omega_1$-level
combinatorics: whether a coherent system of forced meets and joins can be
built across an uncountable index set without collapsing the essential
pattern into something a single point can witness after all.

\begin{remark}[Why finite methods cannot reach this]
\label{rem:finite-methods}
Every finite computational or combinatorial technique this programme has
applied — building explicit finite orthomodular lattices of larger and
larger size, checking by exhaustive case analysis whether some assembly of
finitely many compatible pieces can carry an essential pattern, searching
for finite ``repair'' operations that patch a near-miss into a genuine
lattice — has, without exception, produced only \emph{tame} objects
(Proposition~\ref{prop:finite-obstruction}: every finite orthomodular
poset is automatically tame, for the trivial reason that there is no
infinite limit to fail to respect). This is not a series of unlucky
misses; it is forced by the definitions. \textbf{No finite construction
can ever exhibit requirement (iv) failing at the uncountable scale that
requirement (i) demands}, because finiteness makes $\sigma$-additivity
automatic. The genuinely infinitary question — can lattice closure be
achieved, across an uncountable index set, without ever realizing the
pattern at a single point — is simply not a question that any finite
search, however large, can settle either way. This is why the problem has
resisted direct construction: the tools available for exploring finite
and countable structures are, provably, the wrong tools for this
particular question.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The current assessment}

\begin{remark}[Where the evidence leans — an assessment, not a theorem]
\label{rem:assessment}
Based on everything established so far, the honest assessment is that the
evidence leans toward Conjecture~\ref{conj:main} being \textbf{true} (no
lattice witness exists), though this is emphatically an assessment, not
a proof, and it rests on the following basis: (a) the two-cell
countable-meet obstruction (Proposition~\ref{prop:finite-obstruction}),
which rules out the most natural gluing construction independently of any
centrality assumption; (b) the countably-generated boundary extension
theorem (Theorem~\ref{thm:ctbly-gen}) together with its standard-Borel
strengthening (Proposition~\ref{prop:ctbly-gen-full}), which together
close off every construction built from only countably much data; (c) a
normality trichotomy (not separately stated above as a numbered result,
but of the same character: attempts to build an uncountable ``hub''
structure with a genuinely non-classical shared boundary, while
preserving $\sigma$-state order-separation throughout, have in every
case examined either forced the shared boundary back into the classical
center of the structure or destroyed order-separation outright); and
(d) every attempt so far to identify a genuinely lattice-theoretic
mechanism that would let an essential pattern survive full lattice
closure has, on inspection, either turned out to require exactly the
Boolean/distributive structure the conjecture is about ruling out, or
has been shown, on careful examination, not to supply the needed
mechanism at all (see Section~7 for two such falsified attempts,
examined and rejected in detail). If Conjecture~\ref{conj:main} is
eventually proved, it would close this entire line of investigation
positively: it would say that requiring genuine lattice structure is, by
itself, already enough to force every finitely coherent finite pattern to
extend $\sigma$-additively — a clean and strong structural theorem in its
own right, independent of any of the constructions above.
\end{remark}

\section{The two open directions, precisely}
% =======================================================================

Two distinct, precisely statable mathematical questions remain open, and
resolving either one — in either direction — would constitute genuine
progress. Both are naturally read with a two-sided framing: either
direction of a proof (the obstruction generalizes / the escape succeeds)
tells you something, either a theorem or a design constraint on any
future witness attempt.

\subsection{Route (a): does the countable-meet obstruction generalize?}

\begin{conjecture}[Generalized countable-meet obstruction]
\label{conj:route-a}
Does the two-cell obstruction of
Proposition~\ref{prop:finite-obstruction} generalize from the specific
Cantor-space-like gluing considered there to \emph{every} faithful,
order-separating, countably-generated ``hub'' structure shared between
two or more blocks of a concrete $\sigma$-complete orthomodular lattice —
i.e., is \emph{every} attempt to identify a shared countably-generated
separating boundary between distinct blocks of a lattice necessarily
inconsistent, by the same countable-meet mechanism, regardless of the
specific combinatorial shape of the gluing?
\end{conjecture}

\emph{If proved:} this would be a genuine step toward
Conjecture~\ref{conj:main}, by eliminating an entire broad class of
candidate constructions (all countably-generated-boundary gluings) at
once, rather than one construction at a time. \emph{If it fails} (some
faithful countably-generated hub structure \emph{does} survive lattice
closure without the countable-meet contradiction firing): the failure
would itself be a genuine discovery, supplying — for the first time — a
concrete design template for a lattice-level witness attempt, built
around whatever specific feature let it escape the obstruction.

\subsection{Route (b): the distributed nonseparating quotient route}

\begin{conjecture}[Distributed nonseparating quotient route]
\label{conj:route-b}
Is there a concrete $\sigma$-complete orthomodular lattice built from an
uncountably-generated boundary structure in which the shared boundary
data is \emph{not} required to order-separate points across the whole
structure — i.e., one that evades
Theorem~\ref{thm:ctbly-gen}'s hypothesis (which requires
order-separation) by working instead with a distributed family of
\emph{nonseparating} quotient copies of a shared structure, glued so that
no single global $\sigma$-state can be pieced together across the whole
family, while every finite and countable sub-piece remains individually
consistent?
\end{conjecture}

\emph{If proved} (such a structure can be built as a genuine lattice): this
would refute Conjecture~\ref{conj:main} outright and supply the first
lattice-level witness, closing the poset/lattice gap from the other side.
\emph{If it can be shown impossible} (every attempt at such a distributed
nonseparating gluing, when forced to be a genuine lattice, either
collapses to order-separation after all or fails to remain
$\sigma$-complete): this would be the second and complementary half of a
full proof of Conjecture~\ref{conj:main}, alongside a positive resolution
of Route (a).

\begin{remark}[The two routes as complementary halves]
Routes (a) and (b) are naturally read together: (a) attacks the
\emph{countably-generated} case in full generality; (b) attacks the
genuinely \emph{uncountably-generated, nonseparating} case that (a)
alone does not cover. A full proof of Conjecture~\ref{conj:main} would
most naturally proceed by closing both simultaneously (every countably
generated hub fails by (a)'s mechanism; every uncountably-generated
attempt fails by some extension of the ideas behind (b)'s impossibility
direction). A witness, if one exists at all, must in particular refute
route (b) constructively while consistently already being ruled out by
nothing resembling route (a)'s mechanism.
\end{remark}

\section{What is heuristic, not yet mathematics}
% =======================================================================

Honesty about the boundary between established mathematics and intuition
requires flagging, explicitly, several ideas that were seriously explored
and did \emph{not} survive scrutiny, so that a reader does not later
mistake a dead idea for a live one.

\begin{falsified}[A ``dimensional-equivalence'' relation as a proxy for
shared-boundary agreement]
\label{fals:perspectivity}
One natural-seeming heuristic, motivated loosely by the external boundary
result of Section~4, conjectured that a specific order-theoretic relation
between atoms of an orthomodular lattice — informally, ``these two atoms
are related by a dimension-preserving symmetry of the structure,'' the
lattice-theoretic analogue of Murray--von Neumann equivalence of
projections in an operator algebra — might serve as a faithful proxy for
the entirely different relation of ``these two events are forced to agree
under every state that assigns them both value $1$ on a shared boundary''
(the relation actually doing the work in the assembly obstructions of
Section 3). \textbf{This was tested directly and falsified.} On the
pentagon (Example~\ref{ex:pentagon}) and on finite homogeneous
orthomodular lattices generally, the dimensional-equivalence relation
turns out to be \emph{completely non-selective}: every atom is related to
every other atom, without exception, purely as a structural consequence of
finite homogeneity — any two atoms in such a lattice can always be
connected by a chain of complementary pairs. A relation that relates
\emph{everything} to everything cannot possibly serve as a faithful proxy
for a relation (shared-boundary agreement) that is supposed to be
selective — picking out specific structured pairs and not others. The two
relations are, moreover, of fundamentally different type: one is
rank-sensitive (a property of how projections compare in size or
dimension), the other is rank-blind (a property of which events happen
to agree under a shared state, regardless of any notion of size). No
finite example can rescue this identification, because the
non-selectivity is a structural feature of finite homogeneous lattices
generally, not an accident of the particular examples tested. This
heuristic is dead; it should not recur in future attempts under a new
name.
\end{falsified}

\begin{falsified}[A parity/winding invariant tracking a would-be
obstruction]
\label{fals:winding}
A related heuristic conjectured that some integer- or parity-valued
``winding'' invariant, attached to how a proposed lattice-completion
process forces meets and joins around a cycle of incompatible events
(as in the pentagon, Example~\ref{ex:pentagon}), might detect exactly
when a forced completion is compatible with an essential pattern
surviving versus when it is not — the hoped-for mechanism for making
requirement (iv) of Section~5 mathematically tractable. This heuristic
was built directly on top of the dimensional-equivalence proxy just
falsified (Falsified Claim~\ref{fals:perspectivity}), and falls with it:
once the underlying relation it was meant to track is shown to carry no
selective information, the invariant built from it carries none either.
Moreover, the two natural test objects available — the finite pentagon
(which, being finite, is automatically tame by
Proposition~\ref{prop:finite-obstruction}, so it can only ever
\emph{illustrate}, never genuinely test, a forced-closure phenomenon) and
the one proved witness of Theorem~\ref{thm:main} (which, by
Proposition~\ref{prop:not-lattice}, is provably \emph{not} a lattice, so
it has no forced meets to have a winding invariant \emph{about} in the
first place) — are, for structural reasons, incapable of ever exhibiting
the phenomenon the invariant was designed to detect, regardless of how
the invariant itself is defined. This is a second, independent reason the
idea does not survive: even setting aside Claim~\ref{fals:perspectivity},
there is currently no finite or countable object on which the
hoped-for invariant could ever be observed to do anything at all.
\end{falsified}

\begin{heuristic}[A legitimate, still-unexplored forwarding lead]
\label{heur:z2states}
One genuine mathematical object, distinct from the falsified ideas above
and not yet investigated by this programme in any depth, is worth naming
explicitly as a place a parity-type idea \emph{might} legitimately live:
the study, in the published literature on orthomodular structures closed
under a symmetric-difference operation, of $\mathbb{Z}_2$-valued states —
states valued in the two-element group rather than in $\{0,1\}$ read as
real numbers, studied for their own sake as an algebraic invariant of such
structures. This is a real, previously studied class of objects in the
literature (distinct in kind from the ad hoc winding invariant of
Falsified Claim~\ref{fals:winding}, which was invented for this
programme's purposes and does not appear in the literature under any
name). Whether these $\mathbb{Z}_2$-valued states have anything to do
with the essential-witness question of this document is entirely open
and unexplored; it is flagged here only as a legitimate place to look
next, not as a result, a partial result, or even a properly formulated
conjecture yet.
\end{heuristic}

\section*{Closing remark}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Closing remark}

The problem, in full, is this. A witness to genuinely contextual,
$\sigma$-inextendable probability data has been constructed and proved,
unconditionally, in ZFC — but only on a structure one order-theoretic
notch weaker than the one the field ultimately cares about (an
orthomodular poset rather than a genuine lattice). Whether the extra
requirement of latticehood is, by itself, already enough to force every
such contextual pattern to dissolve is the open question. Three
independent obstruction results and one recent external theorem in an
adjacent world all point the same direction, toward the conjecture being
true, but the actual reason the obstruction is hard to prove outright is
structural and precise: it is a genuinely infinitary,
$\omega_1$-combinatorial question about whether an uncountable family of
forced lattice meets and joins can be assembled coherently without ever
collapsing the pattern to a single point, and no finite method — however
large or clever — is even in principle capable of reaching a question of
that shape. Two precise routes remain open, stated in Section~6; either
one's resolution, in either direction, would be genuine progress on a
question that currently sits exactly at the boundary between what is
known and what is not.

\textbf{The question.} When do local views, each correct as far as it goes, add
up to one world? And when they do not, what are they still telling us?

\smallskip
Part~I gives the mathematical and philosophical core. Part~II asks where the
question is lived: in ecosystems with no species on top, and in societies with
no one in charge. Part~II is a lens, not an application, and
Section~\ref{sec:distance} says plainly how far the mathematics reaches.
Status labels: \st{Lean} machine-checked, \st{hand} proved by hand, otherwise
conjecture.
\end{mdframed}

\part*{Part I. The essence}

\section{The stance}
Answers come first. Questions, and patterns of answers to them that are
consistent, are taken as primitive. A \emph{world} is something answers have to
\emph{earn} by fitting together. It may be an actual point, or an ideal point at
infinity. Nothing is assumed to stand behind the answers except the bare space of
possibilities.

The patterns studied here are \emph{complete} (every question that can be asked
gets a yes or a no) and \emph{coherent} (answers to disjoint questions add up).
In ordinary logic, complete and coherent always means there is a world: the
completeness theorem~\cite{godel1930}. That relies on every ``and'' being
askable. The theory studies what happens when it is not, and so it studies one
gap:
\begin{center}\emph{complete and coherent, yet about no world.}\end{center}

\section{The picture}
Penrose's staircase~\cite{penrose1991}. Each flight of stairs is a
\emph{query}: a view of two landings that says ``this one is higher''. (Physicists
call such a view a \emph{measurement}, and the pattern of overlapping views a
\emph{measurement scenario}.) Every flight goes up,
and the flights close into a loop. Each view is fine on its own, but no building
satisfies all of them. The \emph{questions you can ask} are the questions about
one flight at a time, and anything built from those by ``not'' and by joining
questions that never both say yes. Joint questions across flights are not
automatically askable. Formally this collection is the \emph{carrier}, in the
literature a $\sigma$-class or concrete logic~\cite{navaraptak1983}.

\section{What is known}

\noindent\textbf{Theorem.} \emph{Coherent answers can be about no world exactly
when the views form a loop, there are infinitely many possibilities, and answers
add up only finitely.}

\medskip
\noindent\emph{What separates the finite from the infinite case.} A yes-or-no
pattern is about an (ideal) world exactly when its true answers have the finite
intersection property. A pointless pattern says yes to finitely many questions
that are pairwise compatible yet jointly impossible: Specker's seer, realised at
infinity. Finitely many heights cannot do this, and countable adding restores
the property \st{hand}.

\medskip
\noindent In Aristotle's terms~\cite{aristotle}, finite adding is the
\emph{potential} infinite. Demanding the actual, completed infinity forces a world
back into existence. ``Ideal'' and ``pointless'' match the standard notions of
coherent and non-coherent finitely additive states~\cite{bhaskararao1983}. For
ordinary probabilities and finitely many outcomes, the tree/loop line is
classical~\cite{vorobev1962,bfmy1983}, and which patterns the askable questions
catch is the exclusivity principle of contextuality theory
(Section~\ref{sec:finite}). What is offered as new lies on the infinite side.

\section{Why: what the askable questions contribute}
On the triangle (three landings, three flights) the contradiction could only be
exposed by one question: \emph{do the three heights go round the staircase's
way?} That question can never be built, not even in infinitely many steps
\st{Lean}. Two facts do the work.
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \emph{Balance.} Pick two candidate heights for each landing. That gives
    the eight corners of a box. Every buildable question says yes equally often
    on the even and the odd corners.
  \item \emph{The lift.} Raising the lowest landing above the highest never
    changes the answer to a buildable question, because the box joining the two
    configurations has its other six corners going the other way round.
\end{itemize}
So the coherence survives because the one question that would break it is
unaskable. Beyond that one exclusion, whether answers are pointless depends only
on whether the windows' local answers fit together. In every case checked, any
local answers that agree where they overlap extend to every askable question
\st{hand; Lean for the triangle cases}. The nearest precedent is
Drav\'eck\'y and \v{S}ipo\v{s}'s example~\cite{draveckysipos1980} and the work
of Navara and Pt\'ak~\cite{navaraptak1983,navara1984}. There, answers that
add over the whole infinity can be pointless on a smaller collection of
questions, but they do not extend to all the askable ones.

\section{What creates impossibility, and what does not}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \emph{Renaming never does, for yes-or-no answers.} A window that only
    says ``this landing is that one, renamed'' can be contracted away \st{hand,
    via Katětov's lemma~\cite{katetov1967}}. With probabilities this fails:
    a loop of renamings that shifts every value, like ``each landing is one more
    than the last'', can be a twist, whether the values wrap round a finite
    clock or spread evenly over all numbers \st{computation; hand}.
  \item \emph{Motion never does.} Symmetry and walking the stairs only move
    worlds around. The twist lives in the loop, not in the action \st{hand}.
  \item \emph{``Beyond'' can.} A window that draws one landing \emph{beyond} the
    other carries a direction. A loop of such directions is a task that would
    have to finish before it started.
  \item \emph{And beyonds compose.} A triangle whose directions never go round
    can still fail to close. See the next section.
\end{itemize}

\section{The twist is a failure of composition}\label{sec:composition}
A \emph{twist} is local agreement whose composition round a loop does not return
to its start.

\paragraph{Two forward queries force the third.} Call a query \emph{forward} if it
draws its second landing after the first, landing by landing, and each value of
the second landing comes from only finitely many values of the first.
Independent ``beyond'' draws, renamings and steps ``$x_1=x_0+b$'' are all
forward. If the queries on $\{x_0,x_1\}$ and $\{x_1,x_2\}$ are forward, then any
world fitting both is \emph{unique}, and it fixes how $x_0$ relates to $x_2$:
the \emph{composite} of the two queries, an iterated sum of
ultrafilters~\cite{goldberg2025}. So the triangle has a world exactly when its third
query \emph{is} that composite \st{Lean}. Around a longer loop, the closing query
must equal the composite of the others \st{hand}.

\paragraph{What it explains.} The twist is the \emph{holonomy of composition}: the
closing query disagreeing with what the rest compose to.
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \emph{Independent beyonds:} the composite is again ``$x_0$ first'', so
    the twist is a directed cycle (for orientations with a forward path).
  \item \emph{Renamings:} the composite is the composed renaming, so the twist is
    its monodromy (automatically trivial for yes-or-no answers).
  \item \emph{Steps:} the composite of steps $d_{01}$ and $d_{12}$ is their
    ultrafilter sum. A triangle whose third query is an \emph{independent} draw is
    therefore twisted for every non-principal ultrafilter, although nothing goes
    round in a circle \st{hand}.
\end{itemize}
Prior art: the sum of ultrafilters is classical (Frolík), and the pair-level
description of how two ultrafilters glue is Goldberg's~\cite{goldberg2025}. The
cases above look classical one by one; what appears new is the unification.
Queries that are not forward are the open edge.

\section{Probabilities and finite worlds}\label{sec:finite}
So far answers were yes or no. Let them be probabilities: each query reports
how likely each pair of heights is, and the probabilities of the questions you
can ask must add up. A \emph{mixture of real buildings} is what you get by
choosing heights at random.
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \emph{Loops without shortcuts are blind.} On a ring of four or more
    landings the questions you can ask are exactly the questions about one
    query, so any pattern that agrees where queries overlap is allowed, including
    impossible ones. Example, with two heights around a square: ``different,
    different, same, different''. Every query is fine, but going round flips the
    height three times \st{hand; certified example}.
  \item \emph{The triangle fills itself in, up to three heights.} With two or
    three possible heights, every probability pattern on the triangle's questions
    \emph{is} a mixture of real buildings \st{exact computation}. With four heights
    this fails: a sum of local facts taking the values $0$, $1$, $2$ is an extreme
    non-negative witness that no askable question replaces, so a probabilistic
    twist can hide on a finite triangle \st{exact computation + Maharam's
    criterion~\cite{maharam1972}; corrected October 2026}. The threshold is a known
    one: three-way tables of sizes $3\times4\times4$ are the smallest whose margin
    polytope is not ``compressed''~\cite{sullivant2006,ohsugihibi2010}. What the
    note adds is that at this threshold every askable question goes blind to some
    incoherence. Two triangles sharing a flight,
    four landings all joined, and a fan of three triangles behave the same way
    at two heights, while the five-ring and the ``house'' (a square with a roof)
    hide twists \st{evidence}. So in finite worlds shortcuts help but do not settle it: the number of heights
    matters too.
  \item \emph{For yes-or-no answers, idealisation is what turns a shortcut into a
    twist.} With infinitely
    many heights and finite adding, the triangle carries pointless answers
    \st{Lean}. A box-by-box limit of finite real mixtures is always a real
    mixture \st{hand}. For yes-or-no answers, pointlessness needs infinity: with finitely many
    heights every coherent yes-or-no pattern is a point.
  \item \emph{Renaming can twist, with probabilities.} For yes-or-no answers a
    loop of renamings never creates impossibility. With probabilities it can: a
    loop saying ``each landing is one more than the last'' is coherent on each
    query and impossible as a whole, whether the heights wrap round a clock or
    spread evenly over all numbers \st{computation; hand}. So the sharp divide is
    between \emph{yes-or-no} and \emph{probabilistic} answers, not between finite
    and infinite.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{A third kind of twist.} Renamings (gauge) and ``beyond'' (order) are not
the only ways to twist. On a triangle whose heights take three, four and four
values, let each pair of landings say only which combinations are
\emph{allowed}, nine combinations each, all equally likely. Every allowed pair
extends to an allowed triple, so nothing is logically impossible. Yet no
weighting of the triples reproduces the even spreads, and no askable question
detects this \st{exact computation; certified with an integer program}. The
twist is \emph{relational}: it lives in many-to-many constraints that are fine
enough. Composed pair by pair, such answers allow a noticeably larger set of
closing laws than any world does (about a quarter more, by volume, in this
example). The extra laws pass every askable question and almost every summary
check, and show up only along special directions \st{computation}.

\paragraph{The loop count.} Five people sit in a ring, and
each neighbouring pair reports ``we differ'', each person's answer split evenly.
With two possible answers this is impossible: going round an odd ring, someone
must agree with a neighbour, so the number of disagreements is $0$, $2$ or $4$,
never $5$. Yet the reports pass every question you can ask. What exposes them is
the \emph{loop count}, $4-\#\text{disagreements}$. It is never negative in any
world, and the reports give it $-1$. The loop count is a \emph{sum} of local
facts, but no query sees it and it cannot be built from the questions you can
ask.
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \emph{Two ways to be made of local information.} A fact can \emph{add up}
    from local facts (lie in their linear span), or be \emph{built} from them by
    ``not'' and ``either one''. Built implies adds up, never conversely.
  \item \emph{Lemma.} With finitely many possibilities, a probability pattern on
    the questions you can ask is a mixture of real buildings exactly when it gives
    every non-negative sum of local facts a non-negative value. So a hidden twist
    is always exposed by a non-negative sum that cannot be built: either a
    yes-or-no question that adds up but cannot be built (on the four-ring the loop
    count is such a question), or a many-valued one (on the five-ring)
    \st{owned: Maharam~\cite{maharam1972}, Thm~6.1; Horn and
    Tarski~\cite{horntarski1948}; computation on eight small graphs}. Maharam's
    Example~2.1 is the triangle itself, in linear form.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{This is contextuality, and it is known.} The finite side of this
section is owned by the quantum foundations literature.
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \emph{The witnesses.} The loop counts are exactly the tight
    inequalities of the $n$-cycle scenario, $\sum_i\gamma_i\langle
    X_iX_{i+1}\rangle\le n-2$ with an odd number of signs
    $\gamma_i=-1$~\cite{araujo2013}. The four-ring is the CHSH Bell test, and its
    twist ``different, different, same, different'' is the Popescu--Rohrlich box.
  \item \emph{What the askable questions catch.} A pattern that respects the
    questions you can ask never gives more than total probability one to
    outcomes that pairwise contradict each other. That is the \emph{exclusivity
    principle}, also called local orthogonality or consistent
    exclusivity~\cite{cabello2013,fritz2013,abramskybarbosa2021}. On the
    triangle, the four-ring, the five-ring and the house, the two conditions cut
    out exactly the same patterns \st{exact linear programming, two answers per
    landing; not proved in general}.
  \item \emph{Rings hide, triangles do not.} A single $n$-cycle
    Popescu--Rohrlich box obeys the exclusivity principle for every
    $n\ge 4$~\cite{choudharybarbosa2024}, while Specker's triangle violates it.
    That is the finite fact ``rings of four or more hide twists''. Choudhary and
    Barbosa show that several independent copies can expose what one copy hides,
    using finite Ramsey theory.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Where the new ground is.} For yes-or-no answers, a triangle hides a
twist only with infinitely many heights and finite adding \st{Lean}. For
probabilities, hiding already begins at four heights (above), so the new ground
is the yes-or-no, infinite regime. That is the
part of the theory not found in the contextuality literature after the passes so
far. The infinite proof also runs on Ramsey theory, for ultrafilters on triples
of numbers. Whether ``infinitely many values'' and ``many independent copies''
are one lever is open, and it is an analogy, not yet a transfer.

\medskip
\noindent Whether the infinite triangle's questions absorb such a probabilistic renaming
twist depends on \emph{which} questions are askable.
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item If each landing's own value may be asked about, reduce modulo~$2$: the
    question ``do the first and third landings have the same parity, and the
    second the other?'' is askable, and the renaming loop gives it probability
    $-\tfrac12$. The twist is visible \st{hand; exact computation}.
  \item If only relations may be asked about, one difference at a time, the
    askable questions form Drav\'eck\'y and \v{S}ipo\v{s}'s
    class~\cite{draveckysipos1980}, and the twist hides there.
\end{itemize}
Section~\ref{sec:relata} takes this up: which questions are askable is a choice
between relations and relata.

\section{Asynchrony, and relations versus relata}\label{sec:relata}
\paragraph{Shared time forces a world.} Let each view read a process with its
own \emph{clock}: an average over its own epoch, or its own long-run limit. If
every view uses the same clock, their reports are the marginals of one joint
law, so a world exists \st{hand, one line}. A failure to add up therefore needs
\emph{asynchrony}: views without a common clock or scale. Asynchrony is not
enough. In the three-species cycle, stations reading rarity in decades all run
off the cycle's lap counter, a hidden shared clock, and a world is forced
\st{hand}.

\paragraph{Clocks that cannot be shared.} Relativity forbids a shared clock round
a rotating loop. Synchronise clocks station by station round the equator and the
loop does not close, by about $207$\,ns ($2\Omega A/c^2$; Einstein
synchronisation has holonomy~\cite{minguzzi2005}, and the Sagnac
difference~\cite{rizziruggiero2003} is twice that). GPS resolves it by adopting a
centre: the time of a non-rotating, Earth-centred frame. Read answers first, with
each clock's absolute setting spread evenly, every pair agrees and no world fits.
Only whether the holonomy is non-zero matters \st{hand}. The question becomes
Gudder's~\cite{gudder1969,draveckysipos1980,navaraptak1983,navara1984}:
\emph{does the expectation of a sum equal the sum of the expectations, when the
observables cannot be measured together?}

\paragraph{Relations or relata.} Whether that failure is visible depends on what
may be asked.
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \emph{Relations only}, one at a time (how clock $1$ stands to clock $2$):
    the answers are coherent and about no world. The twist is hidden. This is
    Drav\'eck\'y and \v{S}ipo\v{s}'s example~\cite{draveckysipos1980}.
  \item \emph{Relata allowed} (what clock $1$ itself reads, in units of the
    holonomy): ask whether clocks $1$ and $3$ have the same parity and clock~$2$
    the other. The answers give this probability $-\tfrac12$, so the twist is
    visible \st{hand; exact computation}.
\end{itemize}
The same holds for rankings. Three pairwise majorities that run in a cycle
(Condorcet) pass every question built from comparisons, even combined with
questions about each candidate's own value: with three or more possible values,
every such question is about a single pair, so no pair-by-pair record can be
contradicted by it \st{proved; core Lean-checked}. Questions about
each candidate's own score expose the cycle when the scores wrap round
(a finite reduction carries the twist), and not when they run off to infinity
\st{hand; Lean}. Asking about relata presupposes them, and when no world exists
that presupposition is what turns a quiet incoherence into a visible one. This
is a reading of two cases, not yet a theorem.

\section{The lineage}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \emph{Specker}~\cite{specker1960}: a seer's three boxes, any two of
    which can be opened consistently but not all three. That is the triangle,
    and the seed of the Kochen--Specker theorem~\cite{kochenspecker1967}. The
    sheaf-theoretic account of contextuality~\cite{abramskybrandenburger2011}
    and the classification of cycle scenarios~\cite{kharoofipekokay2024} own the
    finite, ordinary-probability side, and the exclusivity
    principle~\cite{cabello2013,fritz2013} says which finite twists the askable
    questions catch.
  \item \emph{Penrose}~\cite{penrose1991}: impossible figures as a property of
    loops. Here the loop is necessary, and the obstruction is the way steps
    compose.
  \item \emph{Kant}~\cite{kant}: the completed totality of conditions is not
    \emph{given} but \emph{set as a task}. An ideal point is that task, and a
    loop is where the task becomes circular.
\end{itemize}

\part*{Part II. The lived}

\section{The lizards}
On the hills of central California, male side-blotched lizards come in three
throat colours, each with its own strategy. Orange males hold large territories
and take mates from blue males. Blue males guard their mates closely and so beat
the yellow males. Yellow males sneak into orange territories and beat the
oranges. Orange beats blue, blue beats yellow, yellow beats orange. The
frequencies cycle over the years, and no colour wins~\cite{sinervolively1996}.

That has the \emph{shape} of Penrose's staircase: every flight goes up, the
stairs close into a loop, and there is no top. But the lizards' loop is a loop
of \emph{relations} (who beats whom), measured as a payoff
game~\cite{sinervolively1996}. The theory's twists live in loops of
\emph{views}: observers who each see two variables and report a pattern. The
published lizard data contain no such views, so they cannot be tested for a
twist; that would need data collected as pairwise views. Similar cycles are documented among
toxin-producing strains of \emph{E.\ coli}, where the cycle keeps all three
strains alive when interactions are local~\cite{kerr2002}, and among encrusting
organisms on coral reefs~\cite{bussjackson1979}. Network models proposed that
such cycles can sustain many more species than a ranking
could~\cite{allesinalevine2011}. A later replication found the effect much
weaker~\cite{larsenhaine2015}, so the strong claim is contested.

Ordinary logic says a cycle of ``beats'' is a contradiction, because no ranking
satisfies it. Ecology shows it can be an order. It is just not a
\emph{ranking}. That is the shape of the central finding of Part~I: coherent
answers with no single world behind them.

Others have read this cycle non-classically. Aerts, Sinervo and coauthors
report a violation of Bell's inequality in the lizards' mating
competition~\cite{aerts2014}. Their pairwise probabilities come from a fitted
model rather than direct measurement, and a violated marginal law is what
contextuality-by-default calls direct influence, not contextuality. No one, as
far as we found, has run a test of whether a single joint description
\emph{exists} for such a cycle's pairwise data. That test is the honest version
of the question, and it is open.

\section{Ecology: order from local relations}
\paragraph{Cycles of dominance.} An ecosystem does not consult a global
ranking. Each encounter is local: this lizard, that territory, this season.
When local relations form a \emph{tree}, they always add up to one global order.
When they form a \emph{loop}, they can be coherent with no global order at all.

\paragraph{Steps across scales.} Holling's \emph{panarchy}~\cite{panarchy2002}
describes nested adaptive cycles at different speeds: a leaf, a stand, a
landscape. The finding that beyonds compose rhymes with this. The question is not
only whether influences go round in a loop, but whether they \emph{compose}
across scales. It is a rhyme, not yet a theorem about ecosystems.

\paragraph{Partial observation.} Ecological knowledge is assembled from plots,
transects, traps and seasons, each a local view. Do my surveys connect in a
loop? If not, local agreement is enough. If so, they can agree pairwise and
still describe no single landscape.

\section{Anarchism: order without a sovereign}
\paragraph{The stance is already anarchist.} The theory takes \emph{answers
first}. There is no presupposed ground and no sovereign ``real world'' that the
answers report on. A single world, or a single centre, is something local
answers must \emph{earn}. This mirrors an anarchist account of order: it is not
handed down from a centre, it is made, or not made, from relations.
Feyerabend's ``epistemological anarchism''~\cite{feyerabend1975} makes the same
refusal for science.

\paragraph{Hierarchies are trees.} Local agreements arranged as a tree always
add up to one global order. A chain of command is a tree, which is why
hierarchies \emph{look} coherent: structurally, they cannot fail to be. The cost
is that coherence is bought by removing every relation that does not route
through the centre.

\paragraph{Federations are loops.} Kropotkin was a field naturalist before he
was an anarchist. In \emph{Mutual Aid}~\cite{kropotkin1902} he argued from
animals, villages, guilds and medieval cities that cooperation built from local
relations is as real a force in evolution as competition. Elinor Ostrom made
the case empirically~\cite{ostrom1990}. Swiss alpine pastures, Japanese village
forests, Spanish irrigation huertas and Philippine irrigation societies
governed shared resources for centuries with no state or market in charge. One
of her design principles is \emph{nested enterprises}: overlapping local centres
of rule-making. Her \emph{polycentric governance}~\cite{ostrom2010} generalises
it. In the theory's terms these are webs with loops. Pointless coherence is,
structurally, \emph{order without a sovereign}.

\paragraph{What a centre asks for.} A web of relations can cohere with no
underlying table of positions behind it. Demanding relata (a ranking, a common
clock, each party's standing in itself) is what can turn such an order into a
visible contradiction, and adopting a centre, as GPS does, is one way to restore
a world. Both are choices about which questions to ask. This is a reading of the
clock and ranking cases (Section~\ref{sec:relata}), not a theorem about
societies.

\paragraph{How commitments chain.} Local agreements can be fine, even pairwise,
yet fail to add up. That happens not because anyone contradicts anyone, but
because the agreement between the first and last parties must match the
composite of those in between.

\section{Cases: testing the ideas against the world}\label{sec:cases}
The ideas are held to concrete cases before any generalisation, and the cases
can push back.
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \emph{Three species that never settle.} In a rock-paper-scissors cycle of
    competitors~\cite{maylonard1975}, long-run averages never converge; they
    circle a polygon forever~\cite{gaunersdorfer1992,takens1994}. Three field
    stations, each watching one pair of species over its own span of time, can
    agree wherever they overlap and still imply that ``no species is dominant''
    has probability $-0.2$: no long-run world fits them all \st{simulation and
    exact geometry}. Each long-run world exists only as an ideal limit, and there
    is a whole loop of them; the observer's choice of time span selects one.
  \item \emph{One quantity in time windows.} Watching one quantity through
    overlapping time windows never produced the failure: windows that share the
    same variable share too much to disagree \st{simulation}.
  \item \emph{Three bodies.} Pairwise observers of the three-body problem could
    describe no world only along motions whose statistics never settle. How common
    those are is tied to a famous open question of Kolmogorov. In an isolated
    three-body universe each body's own view of its two relations forms the
    triangle, but Newtonian shared time forces a world \st{hand}.
  \item \emph{Rarity in the long run.} Let the field stations read how many
    decades down each species is. That number is unbounded only in the
    continuous, infinite-population model; real populations go
    extinct~\cite{barendregtthomas2023}. The stations' reports all run off the
    cycle's lap counter, which acts as a shared clock, and for every station that
    reads a fixed fraction of each lap a world is forced \st{hand; the rest open}.
  \item \emph{Clocks round the Earth.} Relativity forbids a shared clock, and the
    synchronisation loop fails by $207$\,ns (Section~\ref{sec:relata}). Visible
    if a clock's own reading may be asked about, hidden if only relations may
    \st{hand; exact computation}.
\end{itemize}
The pattern so far, which is a description and not yet a law: no world needs
views of separate variables, statistics that never settle, and observers who
cannot share a clock or way of averaging. Shared time forced a world in every
case that had it; relativity is the case where nature forbids it.

\section{The four questions, lived}

Part~I works with idealised infinities. With finitely many possibilities its own
theorem says every coherent pattern \emph{is} a world. The lizard cycle is
coherent as a \emph{dynamics} rather than as a set of answers, and real commons
are finite. So the lived reading needs a different bridge: a
\emph{probabilistic} or \emph{dynamical} version, in which ``locally consistent
but globally unrealisable'' is real for finite systems. Belief propagation on
loopy networks is one such setting~\cite{wainwrightjordan2008}.

The finite version (Section~\ref{sec:finite}) already changes the lived picture.
Loops without shortcuts, such as a ring of four or more parties with no
cross-links, can hold locally coherent arrangements that no whole supports.
Shortcuts help but do not make a web tree-like: with four or more possible
values, even a triangle can hold such an arrangement (corrected October 2026).
For a commons or a food web, the structural question is not only whether its
loops have shortcuts, but how fine-grained the local states are. Whether the composition law has a
finite, probabilistic shadow is open, and it may be the bridge that matters
most.

That finite bridge is real, and it is not ours: it is contextuality
(Section~\ref{sec:finite}). This is good news for practice. The loop counts are
Bell-type inequalities, and they can be computed from field data. Each survey
that sees one pair supplies one term, and a negative loop count says no single
landscape fits the surveys. The gap to applicability is that each term needs
paired observations over a comparable span, and real data are noisy, so a
violation must be large to mean anything.

\section{What the lived side asks of the mathematics}
Questions 1--3 are the current priorities (October 2026). Question 4 waits on
them.
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{(priority)} \emph{A finite, probabilistic version.} When are local marginals on a
    web of relations consistent yet unrealisable? Does composition, and not only
    cycles, govern it? \emph{Partly answered:} the finite probabilistic version is
    contextuality, and the loop counts are its tests (Section~\ref{sec:finite}).
    Composed pair by pair, answers determine a set of closing laws. Below the
    $3\times4\times4$ threshold the askable questions allow exactly the realisable
    ones; above it they allow more, and the excess is hidden incoherence.
  \item \textbf{(priority)} \emph{Scale as the source of ``beyond''.} In the theory, ``beyond every
    number'' creates twist. In ecology and society the analogue may be
    \emph{separation of scales}. Can that be made precise? \emph{Partly answered:}
    for probabilities, fineness alone suffices: hidden incoherence appears once
    two relations distinguish four values and the third three.
  \item \textbf{(priority)} \emph{Dynamics, revisited.} The theory found that motion only moves
    worlds. The lizard cycle is a \emph{dynamic} order. Can a cycle in time,
    rather than in the questions, carry coherence without a top?
  \item \emph{Design.} Ostrom asked which institutions hold together. Could the
    theory say which webs of local rules can cohere without a centre, and which
    must fail by composition?
\end{enumerate}

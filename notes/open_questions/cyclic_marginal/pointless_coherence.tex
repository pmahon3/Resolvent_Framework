\bp{\large Answers can be consistent without being about anything, as long as
the contradiction hides in a question nobody can ask.}

\medskip
\noindent This note follows that sentence through three small puzzles and out
the other side into a theory. The puzzles come first, because the theory is
only a careful account of what they show. Precise statements sit in short
\emph{Precisely} asides and in the appendix, where proofs and status labels
live (\st{Lean} machine-checked, \st{hand} my proof, \st{cited}
literature).

\section{A die that cannot be thrown}\label{sec:die}

Six faces, and four yes/no questions about which face is up:
\[
\text{in } \{1,2,3\}? \qquad \text{in } \{3,4,5\}? \qquad \text{in } \{5,6,1\}? \qquad \text{in } \{2,4,6\}?
\]
You may also ask the opposite of any of them (``in $\{4,5,6\}$?'', and so on).
That is all. Now suppose someone answers \emph{no} to all four.

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}
\foreach \k in {1,...,6} \coordinate (d\k) at ({90-60*(\k-1)}:1.15);
\draw[line width=6pt,orange!35,line cap=round] (d1)--(d2)--(d3);
\draw[line width=6pt,green!35,line cap=round] (d3)--(d4)--(d5);
\draw[line width=6pt,violet!30,line cap=round] (d5)--(d6)--(d1);
\foreach \k in {1,...,6} \node[var,minimum size=6mm] at (d\k) {\k};
\foreach \k in {2,4,6} \draw[thick,densely dashed,gray] (d\k) circle (4.2mm);
\node[font=\scriptsize,align=left,anchor=west] at (1.9,0.2) {bands: $\{1,2,3\},\{3,4,5\},\{5,6,1\}$};
\node[font=\scriptsize,align=left,anchor=west] at (1.9,-0.25) {dashed: $\{2,4,6\}$};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

\noindent Is that a consistent set of answers? The only rule we can check is
that a question and its opposite get one ``yes'' between them, and they do.
So the answers are \emph{consistent}.

Is there a face that \emph{fits} them, a face that would give exactly these
answers if it were the one showing? No. Every face lies in one of the first
three sets (they cover the die), so every face would answer ``yes'' somewhere.

Where did the contradiction go? A fitting face would have to lie in all four
opposites at once, $\{4,5,6\}\cap\{6,1,2\}\cap\{2,3,4\}\cap\{1,3,5\}$, which is
empty. But ``is it in $\{4,5,6\}$ \emph{and} in $\{6,1,2\}$?'' is not a question
on this board. Nobody is allowed to ask it. The contradiction lives in a
question that cannot be asked.

This is the whole phenomenon in miniature. Here it is harmless, because there
are only finitely many questions. Nothing about it involves infinity, and
nothing more can go wrong.\footnote{The example is from Navara and Pt\'ak, \emph{\v{C}as.\ p\v{e}st.\ mat.}\ 108 (1983).}

\section{Numbers seen two at a time}\label{sec:windows}

Now a board with infinitely many possibilities. There are slots
$x_0,x_1,x_2,\dots$, each of which \emph{could} hold a natural number. Nothing
says they do; we only have questions. Questions come through \emph{windows},
two slots at a time. Through the window on $x_0,x_1$ you may ask anything about
that pair: ``is $x_0<x_1$?'', ``is $(x_0,x_1)=(3,7)$?''. As on the die, you may
build new questions from these with ``not'' and with ``exactly one of these
exclusive options'', even infinitely many options, as in ``which value is
$x_0$?''. A set of answers is \emph{consistent} if, whenever a question is split
into exclusive options, the ``yes''es add up correctly.

Put the windows in a line: $\{x_0,x_1\}$, $\{x_1,x_2\}$, $\{x_2,x_3\}$, and let
each say ``the right number is bigger''.
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[x=1.7cm]
\foreach \i in {0,1,2} \draw[win] (\i,0)--(\i+1,0);
\foreach \i in {0,...,3} \node[var] (n\i) at (\i,0) {$x_\i$};
\foreach \i/\j in {0/1,1/2,2/3} \draw[up] (n\i) -- node[above=3pt,font=\scriptsize]{up} (n\j);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
\noindent The plain way to answer is to pick numbers, say $1,2,3,4$, and read
every answer off them. Those answers are fitted by an actual assignment.

There is a stranger way, which matters for everything that follows. Answer
every question about a single slot as if its number were \emph{infinitely far
out}. ``Is $x_0=5$?'' No. ``Is $x_0=6$?'' No. No to every particular value. ``Is
$x_0$ bigger than a million?'' Yes. ``Is $x_0$ even?'' Some fixed yes or no,
decided once and for all. Such a consistent rule for answering every question
about one number is an \emph{ideal number}.

\precisely{an ideal number is a non-principal ultrafilter $p$ on $\bbN$. Its
existence needs a little of the axiom of choice.}

Now answer a window's questions by \emph{drawing} its two numbers from the same
ideal rule, the right one \emph{later}: first $x_0$, then $x_1$ from even
further out. ``Is $x_0<x_1$?'' Yes, since whatever $x_0$ is, almost every later
draw beats it. Along a line this always fits together. One ideal recipe, ``draw
$x_0$, then $x_1$ later, then $x_2$, then $x_3$'', answers every question about
all four slots at once, and each window's answers are just its answers about
two of them. Call such a recipe for all the slots together an \emph{ideal
point}. An actual assignment is an ideal point too: the trivial recipe that
always draws the same numbers.

\precisely{``draw $x_0$, then $x_1$ later'' is the ordered product $p\otimes p$:
a question $A$ about $(x_0,x_1)$ gets ``yes'' when $p$ says yes to the set of
$x_0$ for which $p$ says yes to $\{x_1:(x_0,x_1)\in A\}$. An ideal point is an
ultrafilter on the whole space of possibilities.}

On a line, then, consistent answers always come from somewhere: an actual
assignment or, at worst, an ideal one. That turns out to be a theorem, not an
accident (\S\ref{sec:why}).

\section{Closing the loop: the staircase}\label{sec:stair}

Bend the line into a ring: windows $\{x_0,x_1\}$, $\{x_1,x_2\}$, $\{x_2,x_3\}$,
$\{x_3,x_0\}$. Let every window say ``up''.
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}
\foreach \k/\a in {0/135,1/45,2/-45,3/-135} \coordinate (r\k) at (\a:1.2);
\draw[win] (r0)--(r1); \draw[win] (r1)--(r2); \draw[win] (r2)--(r3); \draw[win] (r3)--(r0);
\foreach \k in {0,...,3} \node[var] (q\k) at (r\k) {$x_\k$};
\draw[up] (q0)--(q1); \draw[up] (q1)--(q2); \draw[up] (q2)--(q3); \draw[up] (q3)--(q0);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
\noindent With actual numbers this is impossible: $x_0<x_1<x_2<x_3<x_0$. It is a
Penrose staircase, every step up and back to the start.

With ideal numbers, look at what each window sees. Each draws its pair from the
ideal rule, right number later. Every window genuinely says ``up''; in the window
$\{x_3,x_0\}$ it is $x_0$ that is drawn later. Two windows that share a slot
agree about it: in both, $x_0$ is the same ideal number, so ``is $x_0$ even?''
gets the same answer from either side.

Can a consistent answer be given to \emph{every} question that can be built from
these windows? Yes \st{Lean}. And the question that would expose the
staircase, ``is $x_0<x_1<x_2$?'', is not among them. It cannot be built from
windows, any more than the die's ``in this set \emph{and} that one?''. Once
again the contradiction is in a question that cannot be asked.

What \emph{cannot} exist is an ideal point: one recipe for all four slots.
Such a recipe answers every question about the slots, including the forbidden
one, and it would have to say yes to $x_0<x_1<x_2<x_3<x_0$.

So the staircase has consistent answers that nothing fits: no assignment, and
not even an ideal one. Call such answers \textbf{pointless}. Each window's
numbers are ideal; the whole has no point at all. The ideal rule gives each
window a direction, a local ``later'', and the loop closes those directions on
themselves.

The die was pointless too, but trivially so, because it was finite. The
staircase is pointless and infinite, and that is what makes it sharp.

\section{What infinity demands}\label{sec:sigma}

Ask the staircase ``which value is $x_0$?'' and split the question into its
infinitely many options: $x_0=0$, $x_0=1$, $x_0=2$, \dots. Each option gets
``no''. The whole question, ``$x_0$ is some number'', gets ``yes''.
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[x=0.85cm]
\draw[thick,-{Stealth}] (-0.3,0)--(8.6,0) node[right,font=\scriptsize]{$\infty$};
\foreach \n in {0,...,7} {\draw (\n,0.08)--(\n,-0.08); \node[below,font=\scriptsize] at (\n,-0.08) {\n};
  \node[above,font=\scriptsize,red!70!black] at (\n,0.08) {no};}
\draw[decorate,decoration={brace,amplitude=4pt},thick] (0,0.55)--(8.4,0.55)
  node[midway,above=4pt,font=\scriptsize,blue!60!black]{``$x_0$ is some number'': yes};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
\noindent Finite splits are fine: ``$x_0\le100$?'' no, ``$x_0>100$?'' yes. But
suppose we demand that the answers add up across \emph{infinitely} many
options too. This is $\sigma$-additivity, the usual rule of probability theory.
Then some single ``$x_0=n$'' must get the yes. Every slot is pinned to an actual
number, and actual numbers cannot climb around a loop.

On these boards, demanding that answers add up over infinitely many options is
the same as demanding that they come from an actual assignment
(Proposition~\ref{prop:sigma}). The demand for a completed reality is exactly
what forbids the staircase.

\section{Why loops: the first ideas of the theory}\label{sec:why}

The three puzzles show the whole mechanism. Separating its parts gives the
theory's first ideas.

\paragraph{Contradictions need questions that cannot be asked.} Consistent
answers respect every ``and'' that is actually on the board: if two questions
and their joint question can all be asked, two yeses force a yes on the joint
question (Lemma~\ref{lem:basic}). So a pattern can fail to be fitted only
through joint questions the board lacks. That is the space pointlessness lives
in.

\paragraph{Room keeps things local.} On the ring of four, windows $\{x_0,x_1\}$
and $\{x_2,x_3\}$ share no slot. That room has a strong consequence: every
buildable question breaks into a question to one window plus a question to its
neighbour (Lemma~\ref{lem:shape}). So consistent answers are completely
determined by what each window says. The same holds for any line, and for any
ring of length at least four.

\paragraph{Lines always glue.} Two neighbouring windows' ideal recipes, agreeing
on their shared slot, always combine into one recipe for all three slots
(Lemma~\ref{lem:amalg}). Along a line you can keep combining, so on a line every
consistent pattern is an ideal point (Theorem~\ref{thm:path}). Nothing
pointless can happen without a loop.

\paragraph{Loops can carry a twist.} Around a loop the recipes may fail to
combine, as the staircase's directions do. Call a family of window recipes
\emph{flat} if it combines into one recipe for everything, and \emph{twisted}
otherwise. On boards with room, a pattern is an ideal point exactly when its
window family is flat (Proposition~\ref{prop:flat}). Pointless patterns are
twisted families that still extend consistently to every buildable question.

\paragraph{Infinity forbids the twist.} Under $\sigma$-additivity every window is
pinned to actual numbers, and actual numbers carry no direction, so there is
nothing to twist.

\paragraph{The map so far.} Putting the boards together:
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{|>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{3.6cm}|>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{3cm}|>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{3.2cm}|>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{3.2cm}|}
\hline
& \textbf{line} & \textbf{loop of 4 or more} & \textbf{triangle}\\
\hline
finitely many values & actual & actual & actual\\
\hline
infinitely many values, answers add up over infinitely many options & actual & actual & actual\\
\hline
infinitely many values, finite adding only & ideal \st{hand} & \cellcolor{orange!20}\textbf{pointless exists} \st{Lean} & \cellcolor{yellow!25}\textbf{open}\\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\noindent The shaded cells are where the theory lives: a loop, infinitely many
possibilities, and only finite adding.

\section{The open edge: the triangle}\label{sec:triangle}

Shrink the loop to three windows, $\{x_0,x_1\}$, $\{x_1,x_2\}$, $\{x_2,x_0\}$.
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}
\foreach \k/\a in {0/90,1/-30,2/210} \coordinate (t\k) at (\a:1.1);
\draw[win] (t0)--(t1); \draw[win] (t1)--(t2); \draw[win] (t2)--(t0);
\foreach \k in {0,1,2} \node[var] (u\k) at (t\k) {$x_\k$};
\draw[up] (u0)--(u1); \draw[up] (u1)--(u2); \draw[up] (u2)--(u0);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
\noindent Now there is no room: every window shares a slot with every other.
Questions can braid all three windows. With two possible values, ``are all three
equal?'' becomes askable, though it cannot be broken into window questions. With
three possible values that question disappears, and 2{,}088 others take its
place. The ring's method, reading every answer off neighbouring windows, no
longer applies.

So the triangle is the experiment that decides what the theory is about. If the
staircase still has consistent answers here, then \emph{any} loop permits
pointless coherence. If it does not, pointlessness needs room as well as a loop.
A computer check on a faithful finite part of the board finds consistent
answers, so the evidence leans toward \emph{any loop}. Nobody has built them on
the whole board.

\paragraph{What the theory must settle.}
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \emph{The invariant.} Which property of the window graph decides whether
    pointless patterns exist: a loop, or a loop with room? The triangle decides.
  \item \emph{The classification.} Which twisted families extend to every
    buildable question? The hope is that twist is measured by something like a
    winding number of the directions around each loop.
  \item \emph{Wider windows.} Windows showing three or more slots at once.
  \item \emph{No background at all.} The boards assume a space of
    \emph{possibilities} (the slots and their possible values) and nothing
    more. Drop even that, keeping only the questions. Does pointlessness
    survive when there is no space to lack a point in?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Coda: where this sits}\label{sec:coda}

\paragraph{Its neighbours.} Each neighbouring theory covers a region and stops
at the edge of the shaded cells.
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \emph{Lines.} Vorob'ev (1962) and database theory show that consistent
    data on line-like structures always comes from a whole, for ordinary
    probabilities.
  \item \emph{Loops, with ordinary probabilities and finitely many outcomes.}
    The sheaf-theoretic account of contextuality owns this: Abramsky and
    Brandenburger, and for loops exactly, Kharoof, Ipek and Okay (2024).
  \item \emph{Infinity on ordinary (Boolean) boards.} De Finetti and Howson. On
    a board where every ``and'' can be asked, consistent answers are always ideal
    points.
\end{itemize}
None of them covers finitely additive answers on glued boards. That gap is
where this theory sits.

\paragraph{Its lineage.} The picture is old.
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \emph{Specker} opened the paper that became the Kochen--Specker theorem
    (1960) with a seer's three boxes. Any two opened hold exactly one gem, which
    is pairwise consistent and jointly impossible. That is the triangle.
  \item \emph{Penrose} explained the impossible staircase and tribar by the
    cohomology of a loop (1991).
  \item \emph{Aristotle} held that the infinite exists only potentially, never
    as a completed whole. Finite adding is the potential infinite. Adding up
    over infinitely many options is the actual one, and the staircase is a case
    where demanding the actual infinite excludes what the potential allows.
  \item \emph{Kant} called the completed series of conditions not \emph{given}
    but \emph{aufgegeben}, set as a task: ``nicht als Axiom, die Totalit\"at im
    Objekt als wirklich zu denken, sondern als ein Problem f\"ur den Verstand''.
    The staircase is a small antinomy. Every window is right, and the totality
    read as an object contradicts them, while read as a task it does not.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Its stance.} Throughout, answers come first. Nothing is assumed to
be giving them. Only the space of possibilities is assumed. Points, actual or
ideal, are what we get when answers can be reconstructed into a whole. The
theory is the study of when that reconstruction succeeds, and of what remains
when it fails.

\section{Definitions and results}\label{app:defs}

\begin{definition}[boards and answers]
A \emph{possibility space} is $\Omega=X^V$, with $V$ finite and $X$ an alphabet.
A \emph{window} is a two-element $\{i,j\}\subseteq V$, and the windows form a
graph. A \emph{window question} is $\{x:(x_i,x_j)\in A\}$ with $A\subseteq X^2$
arbitrary. The \emph{carrier} $\cD$ is the smallest family containing all window
questions and closed under complement and countable disjoint union: the
buildable questions. A \emph{state} (consistent answers) is
$s:\cD\to\{0,1\}$ with $s(\Omega)=1$ and $s(A\sqcup B)=s(A)+s(B)$ for disjoint
$A,B$. It is \emph{$\sigma$-additive} if additivity holds for countable disjoint
unions.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[points]
A state is a \emph{point} (fitted by an actual assignment) if
$s(E)=[\omega\in E]$ for some $\omega\in\Omega$. It is an \emph{ideal point} if
$s(E)=[E\in W]$ for an ultrafilter $W$ on $\Omega$. It is \emph{pointless} if it
is neither.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:basic}\st{hand; standard}
Let $s$ be a state on $\cD$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),nosep]
  \item If $A$, $B$ and $A\cap B$ are in $\cD$ and $s(A)=s(B)=1$, then
    $s(A\cap B)=1$.
  \item $s$ is an ideal point iff its true sets have the finite intersection
    property.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),nosep]
  \item $A\setminus B=(A\cap B^c)$ lies in $\cD$, being $A$ minus $A\cap B$, and
    lies inside $B^c$. So $s(A\setminus B)\le s(B^c)=0$.
  \item Extend the true sets to an ultrafilter; the false sets have true
    complements.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:sigma}\st{Lean: ring, \texttt{no\_sigma\_extension};
hand: general}
If $X$ is countable, every $\sigma$-additive state on a glued carrier is a
point. If $X$ is finite, every state is a point.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item On each window the fibres $\{(x_i,x_j)=(a,b)\}$ form a countable (or
    finite) partition, so exactly one is true.
  \item Shared coordinates make these choices agree, giving one $\omega$.
  \item The sets on which $s$ agrees with $\delta_\omega$ form a carrier
    containing the window questions, hence all of $\cD$.
\end{itemize}
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[room]\label{lem:shape}\st{hand; checked on finite alphabets}
If no three windows pairwise share a coordinate, every $E\in\cD$ is
$E_{k-1}\sqcup E_k$ with window questions at two adjacent windows. This applies
to lines and to rings with $L\ge4$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item Window questions at windows with no shared coordinate both meet if
    non-empty.
  \item For $E=E_{k-1}\sqcup E_k$, slicing at the shared coordinate gives
    disjoint cylinders in independent coordinates, so one is empty. Hence
    $E^c$ has the same form.
  \item The family of disjoint unions of one window question per window is
    therefore closed under complement and countable disjoint union, so it is
    $\cD$.
\end{itemize}
On the triangle this fails: with binary values the carrier has 70 sets against
66 (\texttt{pointless\_path\_dynkin.py}, \texttt{triangle\_loop\_questions.py}).
Compare Greechie's loop lemma (Kalmbach 1983, \S4 L9).
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[gluing]\label{lem:amalg}\st{hand; very likely folklore}
Ultrafilters on $X\times Y$ and $Y\times Z$ with the same $Y$-marginal are the
projections of one ultrafilter on $X\times Y\times Z$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Members of the two ultrafilters project into the common marginal, so they meet;
the sets $A\times Z$ and $X\times B$ have the finite intersection property.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[lines]\label{thm:path}\st{hand}
On a line every state is an ideal point.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Glue the window ultrafilters along the line into $W$. Writing
$E=E_{k-1}\sqcup E_k$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:shape}) gives
$s(E)=u_{k-1}(E_{k-1})+u_k(E_k)=W(E)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[flatness]\label{prop:flat}\st{hand}
Under the hypothesis of Lemma~\ref{lem:shape}, a state is an ideal point iff its
window ultrafilters glue to one ultrafilter on $\Omega$ (a flat family).
\end{proposition}

\begin{theorem}[the staircase]\label{thm:witness}\st{Lean: \texttt{mu\_cases},
\texttt{sL\_sigmaEssential}}
For every $L\ge4$, the window ultrafilters $u_k=p\otimes p$ on the ring extend to
a state on the whole carrier over $\bbN^L$. No ultrafilter on $\bbN^L$ projects
to all $u_k$, since every $\{x_k<x_{k+1}\}$ would be in it, and their
intersection is empty. So the state is pointless, and by
Proposition~\ref{prop:sigma} it has no $\sigma$-additive version.
\end{theorem}
\noindent At $L=4$ the carrier's centre is literally $\{\emptyset,\Omega\}$ and
all six admissibility conjuncts hold (Lean, \texttt{D4\_six\_conjuncts}). No
prior-art owner was found in three passes.

\bp{\large Answers can be consistent without being about anything, as long as the
contradiction hides in a question nobody can ask.}

\noindent With finitely many possibilities that is harmless: the die. Around a
loop with infinitely many possibilities it becomes sharp. The consistent answers
exist, but demanding that they add up over infinitely many cases destroys them:
the staircase. Without a loop it cannot happen: the path. This note develops the
theory of when such \emph{pointless} coherence occurs.

\section{Domain and scope}\label{sec:domain}

\paragraph{What the theory is about.} The objects are \emph{glued carriers}:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item a possibility space $\Omega=X^V$: what \emph{could} be the case;
  \item a graph of two-element windows on $V$;
  \item the Dynkin system generated by all questions about single windows.
\end{itemize}
On a glued carrier the theory studies \emph{states}: two-valued, finitely
additive states. Its question is the \textbf{trichotomy}: is a state a point,
an ideal point (an ultrafilter on $\Omega$), or pointless?

\paragraph{Stance: answers first} (adopted 2026-10-16).
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \emph{Primitive:} the questions and a state, i.e.\ a consistent pattern of
    answers. No answerer, substrate or hidden configuration is assumed.
  \item \emph{Derived:} points and ideal points. They are properties a state may
    or may not have, namely that its answers are those of some configuration, or
    of an ideal one. Reconstruction may succeed or fail.
  \item \emph{Pointless states are a region of the domain, not edge cases.} They
    are where reconstruction fails and coherence survives.
  \item \emph{The one thing assumed} is the possibility space $\Omega$: questions
    are sets of possibilities. So possibility is assumed and actuality is earned.
    $\Omega$ is a scaffold, not an ontology. It also gives ``pointless'' its sharp
    meaning: no point \emph{of this} $\Omega$, which the windows fix.
  \item \emph{Dropping $\Omega$ too} (abstract carriers) is a planned extension
    (\S\ref{sec:conj}).
\end{itemize}
Everything below is written to this stance: the puzzle, the definitions and the
Lean development, which defines a state as a function on a Dynkin system over
$\Omega$ and nothing more.

\paragraph{The parameters of the domain.}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Shape} of the window graph: path, forest, ring of length $L$,
    triangle, graphs with chords. Wider windows (three or more coordinates) are a
    planned extension.
  \item \textbf{Alphabet} $X$: finite, countable ($\bbN$), or uncountable.
  \item \textbf{Additivity}: finite, or countable ($\sigma$).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The map so far.} (The boards of the puzzle, Appendix~\ref{sec:puzzle},
are the cells of this map.)

\section{Objects}\label{sec:objects}

\begin{definition}[carrier, state]
A \emph{carrier} on a set $\Omega$ is a family $\cD$ of subsets containing
$\Omega$ and closed under complements and countable disjoint unions (a Dynkin
system). A \emph{state} here always means a two-valued state $s:\cD\to\{0,1\}$ with
$s(\Omega)=1$ and $s(A\sqcup B)=s(A)+s(B)$ for disjoint $A,B\in\cD$. It is
\emph{$\sigma$-additive} if this extends to countable disjoint unions.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[the trichotomy]
A state $s$ is:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item a \emph{point} if $s(E)=[\omega\in E]$ for some $\omega\in\Omega$;
  \item an \emph{ideal point} if $s(E)=[E\in W]$ for some ultrafilter $W$ on
    $\Omega$;
  \item \emph{pointless} otherwise.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[glued carrier, window graph]
Let $\Omega=X^V$ for a finite set $V$ (the possibility space;
\S\ref{sec:domain}, Stance), and let $\mathcal W$ be a set of
two-element \emph{windows} $\{i,j\}\subseteq V$ covering $V$. Together they form
the \emph{window graph}. A \emph{window event} at $\{i,j\}$ is a set
$\{x:(x_i,x_j)\in A\}$ with $A\subseteq X^2$ arbitrary. The \emph{glued carrier}
$\cD$ is the carrier generated by all window events.
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item The \emph{path} has $V=\{0,\dots,n\}$ and windows $W_k=\{k,k{+}1\}$.
  \item The \emph{ring} has $V=\bbZ/L$ and the same windows taken mod $L$.
  \item The \emph{triangle} is the ring with $L=3$.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

The window events at one window form a copy of $\mathcal P(X^2)$, so a state
restricts there to an ultrafilter $u_k$ on $X^2$. Adjacent ones agree on their
shared coordinate. Call $(u_k)$ the state's \emph{window family}.

\begin{definition}[ordered product]
For ultrafilters $p,q$ on $\bbN$: $A\in p\otimes q$ iff
$\{m:\{n:(m,n)\in A\}\in q\}\in p$. Read it as ``draw $m$, then draw $n$ later''.
\end{definition}

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{The basic lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:basic}\st{hand; standard}
Let $s$ be a state on a carrier $\cD$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),nosep]
  \item If $A\subseteq B$ are in $\cD$, then $B\setminus A\in\cD$ and
    $s(A)\le s(B)$.
  \item If $A$, $B$ and $A\cap B$ are all in $\cD$ and $s(A)=s(B)=1$, then
    $s(A\cap B)=1$.
  \item $s$ is an ideal point iff its true sets have the finite intersection
    property.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),nosep]
  \item $B\setminus A=(A\sqcup B^c)^c$.
  \item $A\setminus B\in\cD$ lies inside $B^c$, so it is false by~(a).
  \item Extend the true sets to an ultrafilter $W$. False sets have true
    complements, so they are not in $W$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proof}

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{$\sigma$}{sigma}-additivity forces points}\label{sec:dirac}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:dirac}\st{Lean for the ring:
\texttt{no\_sigma\_extension}; hand for general pair-window graphs}
Over a countable alphabet, every $\sigma$-additive state on a glued carrier is
a point. On the ring, therefore, no $\sigma$-additive state makes every
$C_k=\{x_k<x_{k+1}\}$ true.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item On each window the countably many fibres $\{(x_i,x_j)=(a,b)\}$ partition
    $\Omega$, so exactly one is true.
  \item Shared coordinates make these choices agree, giving one $\omega$.
  \item The sets on which $s$ and $\delta_\omega$ agree form a carrier containing
    the generators, so that carrier is all of $\cD$.
  \item On the ring, all $C_k$ true would give $\omega_0<\dots<\omega_{L-1}<\omega_0$.
\end{itemize}
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:finite}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \emph{Finite alphabets.} The same argument applies with finite additivity
    alone, since the fibre partition is finite. So every state is a point, and
    finite tests of the full carrier cannot detect pointlessness.
  \item \emph{Uncountable alphabets.} The argument fails, since the fibres no
    longer form a countable partition.
\end{itemize}
\st{hand}
\end{remark}

\subsection{Pointlessness alone is cheap}\label{sec:cheap}

\begin{example}[Navara--Pt\'ak 1983]\st{cited; checked by hand}
Take $\Omega=\{1,\dots,6\}$ with $M_1=\{1,2,3\}$, $M_2=\{3,4,5\}$,
$M_3=\{5,6,1\}$ and $M_4=\{2,4,6\}$.
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item The generated carrier is $\{\emptyset,\Omega,M_i,M_i^c\}$.
  \item $s(M_i)=0$ for all $i$ is a state.
  \item It is not a point: $\bigcap_iM_i^c=\emptyset$.
\end{itemize}
\end{example}
\noindent This carrier is not glued from windows. It marks the boundary of the
theory. Pointlessness alone exists finitely, where it is automatically
$\sigma$-additive. What the theory studies is pointlessness that the
$\sigma$-completion \emph{forbids}.

\subsection{The shape of the carrier}\label{sec:shape}

\begin{lemma}[two adjacent windows suffice]\label{lem:shape}\st{hand; checked on
finite alphabets}
Suppose no three windows pairwise share a coordinate. This holds for paths and
for rings with $L\ge4$. Then every $E\in\cD$ is $E_{k-1}\sqcup E_k$, with window
events at two adjacent windows.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \emph{Rigidity.} Non-empty window events at windows with no shared
    coordinate always intersect.
  \item \emph{Slicing.} For $E=E_{k-1}\sqcup E_k$, fix the shared $x_k=c$. The two
    slices are disjoint cylinders in independent coordinates, so one is empty.
    Hence $E^c$ has the same form.
  \item \emph{Closure.} The family $\cG$ of disjoint unions of one window event per
    window has at most two adjacent non-empty pieces, by rigidity. It is closed
    under complements by slicing, and under countable disjoint unions by
    regrouping window by window.
  \item \emph{Conclusion.} $\cG$ is a carrier, so $\cD=\cG$.
\end{itemize}
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \emph{Machine check} (\texttt{notes/open\_questions/verification/pointless\_path\_dynkin.py}):
    $\cD=\cG$ on binary paths of 2--4 windows, the ternary path of 2 windows, and
    the binary 4-ring.
  \item \emph{The triangle} breaks it. With two values, $|\cD|=70$ and $|\cG|=66$.
    With three values, 2{,}088 sets have neither $E$ nor $E^c$ in $\cG$
    (\texttt{triangle\_loop\_questions.py}).
  \item \emph{Comparison.} Greechie's Loop Lemma also singles out order-3 loops
    (Kalmbach 1983, \S4 Lemma~9).
  \item \emph{Consequence on the ring} \st{hand}:
    $C_0\cap C_1=\{x_0<x_1<x_2\}\notin\cD_L$, since its slice at $x_1=c$ is not a
    cylinder.
\end{itemize}
\end{remark}

\subsection{Acyclic: ideal points always}\label{sec:paths}

\begin{lemma}[amalgamation]\label{lem:amalg}\st{hand; very likely folklore}
Ultrafilters $U$ on $X\times Y$ and $V$ on $Y\times Z$ with the same
$Y$-marginal are the projections of one ultrafilter on $X\times Y\times Z$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The projections $\pi_YA$ and $\pi_YB$ lie in the common marginal and so meet.
Hence the sets $\{A\times Z\}\cup\{X\times B\}$ have the finite intersection
property; extend them and use maximality.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[no cycle, no pointless coherence]\label{thm:path}\st{hand}
On the glued path every state is an ideal point.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Amalgamate the window family along the path to an ultrafilter $W$. For $E\in\cD$
write $E=E_{k-1}\sqcup E_k$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:shape}). Then
$s(E)=u_{k-1}(E_{k-1})+u_k(E_k)=W(E)$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Cycles: the staircase}\label{sec:stair}

Fix a non-principal $p$, and put $u_k=p\otimes p$ on each ring window, read on
$(x_k,x_{k+1})$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:pp}\st{hand; standard}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),nosep]
  \item $\{m<n\}\in p\otimes p$.
  \item Both marginals of $p\otimes p$ are $p$.
  \item $p\otimes p$ differs from its transpose.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
\noindent So the $u_k$ form a compatible window family, and every window says
``up''.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:stair}\st{hand}
No ultrafilter on $\bbN^L$ projects to every $u_k$. Moreover, no two-valued state
on the \emph{algebra} generated by the windows agrees with them, since it would
make $\bigcap_kC_k=\emptyset$ true.
\end{proposition}

\begin{theorem}[the countable witness]\label{thm:witness}\st{Lean:
\texttt{mu\_cases}, \texttt{sL\_sigmaEssential}}
For every $L\ge4$ the staircase family extends to a state on the whole glued
carrier $\cD_L$ over $\bbN^L$. That state is pointless
(Proposition~\ref{prop:stair}) and has no $\sigma$-additive version
(Proposition~\ref{prop:dirac}).
\end{theorem}
\noindent \emph{Supplementary Lean receipts at $L=4$}
(\texttt{targetA\_sharp\_countable\_literal}, \texttt{D4\_six\_conjuncts}):
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item the carrier's centre is literally $\{\emptyset,\Omega\}$;
  \item all six admissibility conjuncts hold, with the Polish clause conditional
    as for the $\omega_1$ witness.
\end{itemize}
No prior-art owner was found in three passes.

\subsection{The ring state is forced}\label{sec:forced}

By Lemma~\ref{lem:shape} on rings with $L\ge4$, any state satisfies
\[ s(E)=u_{k-1}(E_{k-1})+u_k(E_k)\qquad(E=E_{k-1}\sqcup E_k). \]
So a state is \emph{determined} by its window family. \st{hand}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \emph{The formula never exceeds 1.} $u_{k-1}$ and $u_k$ amalgamate on
    three consecutive coordinates, so two disjoint true pieces are impossible.
  \item \emph{What remains for} \texttt{mu\_cases}: global well-definedness and
    additivity of this formula.
\end{itemize}

\section{What the proofs say}\label{sec:lessons}

Read as data, the proofs share five elements of simplicity. They are the content
the theory is built from.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Pointlessness lives in missing intersections.} A state respects
    every intersection its carrier contains (Lemma~\ref{lem:basic}). The room for
    pointlessness is exactly the gap between a carrier and the algebra it
    generates, such as $C_0\cap C_1\notin\cD$.
  \item \textbf{Room between windows makes the carrier local.} When no three
    windows meet pairwise, the carrier consists of two-window sets, and a state
    is \emph{the same thing as} a compatible window family that passes an
    extension test (Lemma~\ref{lem:shape}, \S\ref{sec:forced}). The theory
    reduces to the combinatorics of gluing ultrafilters.
  \item \textbf{Acyclic gluing is flat.} Along a tree, compatible window families
    always amalgamate (Lemma~\ref{lem:amalg}). So any obstruction is a failure to
    amalgamate \emph{around a cycle}: a holonomy of ultrafilter gluing.
  \item \textbf{Orientation is the source of holonomy.} $p\otimes p$ differs from
    its transpose, so each window carries an arrow, a local ``later''. Around a
    loop the arrows can wind, and the staircase is the winding family.
  \item \textbf{$\sigma$-additivity collapses windows to points}
    (Proposition~\ref{prop:dirac}). A window that is a point carries no arrow, so
    there is no holonomy. On these carriers the $\sigma$-completion is exactly
    the demand to be made of points.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent These suggest the central notion, offered as a working definition and
not a result.

\begin{definition}[flat and non-flat families; tentative]\label{def:flat}
A compatible window family on a window graph is \emph{flat} if it amalgamates to
an ultrafilter on $\Omega$, and \emph{non-flat} otherwise.
\end{definition}

On graphs where Lemma~\ref{lem:shape} holds, a state is an ideal point iff its
window family is flat, by Theorem~\ref{thm:path}'s argument. Pointless states
are therefore exactly the non-flat families that pass the extension test.
\st{hand, for graphs satisfying Lemma~\ref{lem:shape}}

\section{Conjectures and the target theorem}\label{sec:conj}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The invariant.} For pair-window graphs, which property decides
    whether pointless states exist? The two candidates are \emph{has a cycle}
    and \emph{has a cycle of length $\ge4$}.
    \begin{itemize}[nosep]
      \item \emph{Probe} (pre-registered as \texttt{gate.triangle} in the
        taxonomy): does the triangle carry a pointless staircase state?
      \item If yes: ``any cycle''.
      \item If no: ``room is needed'', and Greechie's order-3 exception bites.
      \item \emph{Evidence}: on a faithful finite sub-carrier the staircase extends
        consistently and two-valued (430 and 74{,}738 sets). The ring formula of
        \S\ref{sec:forced} is not available there, since
        Lemma~\ref{lem:shape} fails.
    \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Forests.} Never pointless. There is a sketch, slicing at a star
    centre and then amalgamating, but it is not written out.
  \item \textbf{Classification (the target structure theorem).} Pointless
    states on a window graph correspond to non-flat compatible families that
    pass the extension test (Definition~\ref{def:flat}). The hope is that
    non-flatness is measured by a first-cohomology-like invariant of the graph,
    with coefficients in ultrafilter orders (winding). This is a target, not a
    claim.
  \item \textbf{Extension test.} Which non-flat families extend to the whole
    carrier? The staircase does. Is every non-flat family on a ring of length
    $\ge4$ extendable?
  \item \textbf{Wider windows.} With windows of width $\ge3$, rigidity weakens.
    What replaces ``acyclic'': Vorob'ev's regularity?
  \item \textbf{Abstract carriers (answers first, all the way down).} Drop
    $\Omega$: take a carrier as an abstract structure of questions with
    exclusivity and countable joins (an orthoposet or test space). Then call a
    state \emph{representable} if some concrete set representation realises it
    as a point or an ideal point. Does pointlessness survive when there is no
    background space to fail to have a point in? Here the stance is Foulis and
    Randall's (owned); the classification would be new.
  \item \textbf{A lens to check.} Deligne's theorem (SGA4) says coherent toposes
    have enough points; this is completeness for coherent logic. Is pointless
    coherence the failure of a Deligne-type theorem for probabilistic coherence?
    \st{unchecked}
\end{enumerate}

\section{The puzzle, board by board}\label{sec:puzzle}

The essence, played out as a game on several boards. Each paragraph ends with the
result it encodes; each board has a walk-through. The dictionary at the end pins
every puzzle word to its exact object.

\paragraph{The game.} There are slots $x_0,x_1,x_2,\dots$, each of which
\emph{could} hold a number. Nothing says they do.
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \emph{Windows.} Questions come through \emph{windows}, a couple of slots
    at a time. Through a window you may ask any question about its slots: ``is $x_0<x_1$?'', ``is $(x_0,x_1)=(3,7)$?''.
  \item \emph{Compound questions.} You may also build questions from those, with
    ``not'' and with ``exactly one of these mutually exclusive options''. That
    allows even infinitely many options, such as ``which value is $x_0$?''.
  \item \emph{The answer pattern.} An \emph{answer pattern} says yes or no to
    every such question. It must be consistent: when a question is split into
    two exclusive parts, exactly the right number of them get a ``yes''.
    Nobody has to be giving the answers; it is just a pattern.
  \item \emph{The question.} Is there an assignment of numbers to the slots, a
    \emph{reality}, whose answers these are? Nothing guarantees one: the answers
    come first.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The path: always fine.} Windows $\{x_0,x_1\},\{x_1,x_2\},\{x_2,x_3\}$,
each saying ``the right number is bigger''. You can comply: $1,2,3,4$. Even with
strange answer patterns, the views always chain together into one whole, at worst an
\emph{ideal} one (see below). \emph{(Theorem~\ref{thm:path}.)}
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[x=1.7cm]
\foreach \i in {0,1,2} \draw[win] (\i,0)--(\i+1,0);
\foreach \i in {0,...,3} \node[var] (n\i) at (\i,0) {$x_\i$};
\foreach \i/\j in {0/1,1/2,2/3} \draw[up] (n\i) -- node[above=3pt,font=\scriptsize]{up} (n\j);
\node[font=\scriptsize,right=4mm] at (n3) {e.g.\ $1,2,3,4$ \ \checkmark};
\end{tikzpicture}\\[-2pt]
{\scriptsize Shaded bands are windows; an arrow means ``the number it points to is bigger''.}
\end{center}

\bp{\textbf{Walking through the path.}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \emph{An ordinary answer pattern.} Take the assignment $1,2,3,4$ and
    answer every question by reading it off: ``is $x_0<x_1$?'' yes; ``is
    $x_2=3$?'' yes; ``is $x_1$ odd?'' no. That pattern is a point, a reality.
  \item \emph{A strange one.} Let each window answer by the far-out rule of the
    ring below: the second slot is ``drawn later, from further out''. No window
    names a value: ``is $x_0=5$?'' gets no, for every number.
  \item \emph{It still describes a whole.} One ideal recipe answers for all four
    slots at once: draw $x_0$ far out, then $x_1$ later, then $x_2$, then $x_3$.
    Each window's answers are that recipe's answers about its two slots. Along a
    line you can always keep drawing in order, so the windows' local orders fit
    one global order. That is the amalgamation lemma at work.
\end{itemize}}

\paragraph{Six outcomes: no single answer, but harmless.} A die with six faces
and four yes/no questions: ``is it in $\{1,2,3\}$, in $\{3,4,5\}$, in
$\{5,6,1\}$, in $\{2,4,6\}$?''. The first three overlap in a loop. Answering
``no'' to all four is perfectly consistent, yet no face gives those answers.
Nothing infinite is involved, so nothing more can go wrong. \emph{(The
Navara--Pt\'ak example, \S\ref{sec:cheap}.)}
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}
\foreach \k in {1,...,6} \coordinate (d\k) at ({90-60*(\k-1)}:1.25);
\draw[line width=6pt,orange!35,line cap=round] (d1)--(d2)--(d3);
\draw[line width=6pt,green!35,line cap=round] (d3)--(d4)--(d5);
\draw[line width=6pt,violet!30,line cap=round] (d5)--(d6)--(d1);
\foreach \k in {1,...,6} \node[var,minimum size=6mm] at (d\k) {\k};
\foreach \k in {2,4,6} \draw[thick,densely dashed,gray] (d\k) circle (4.3mm);
\node[font=\scriptsize,align=left,anchor=west] at (2.0,0)
 {bands: $\{1,2,3\},\{3,4,5\},\{5,6,1\}$\\ dashed: $\{2,4,6\}$\\ answer ``no'' to all four:\\ consistent, but no face fits};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

\bp{\textbf{Walking through the die.}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \emph{Which questions exist.} Only the four questions, their opposites
    (``is it in $\{4,5,6\}$?'', etc.), ``always'' and ``never''. Disjoint
    three-face sets out of six are always opposites, so no new questions can be
    built.
  \item \emph{What consistency demands.} It only applies to a question split into
    two exclusive parts. Here that means a question and its opposite: exactly
    one gets ``yes''.
  \item \emph{The pattern.} Answer ``no'' to $\{1,2,3\},\{3,4,5\},\{5,6,1\},\{2,4,6\}$,
    and so ``yes'' to $\{4,5,6\},\{6,1,2\},\{2,3,4\},\{1,3,5\}$. Every rule is
    satisfied.
  \item \emph{No face gives it.} Each face lands in two of the four sets:
    \begin{center}\small
    \begin{tabular}{c|cccc}
    face & $\{1,2,3\}$ & $\{3,4,5\}$ & $\{5,6,1\}$ & $\{2,4,6\}$\\\hline
    1 & yes & no & yes & no\\
    2 & yes & no & no & yes\\
    3 & yes & yes & no & no\\
    4 & no & yes & no & yes\\
    5 & no & yes & yes & no\\
    6 & no & no & yes & yes\\
    \end{tabular}
    \end{center}
    So no face answers ``no'' to all four. (The first three sets already cover
    the die; the fourth only makes the example symmetric.)
  \item \emph{Why no contradiction ever shows up.} A face fitting all the ``yes''
    answers would lie in $\{4,5,6\}\cap\{6,1,2\}\cap\{2,3,4\}\cap\{1,3,5\}=\emptyset$.
    But ``is it in both $\{4,5,6\}$ and $\{6,1,2\}$?'' is \emph{not a question
    here}: the carrier contains no intersections. The contradiction lives only
    in a question that cannot be asked.
  \item \emph{Why it is harmless.} There are only finitely many questions, so
    ``adding up across infinitely many options'' never arises. Pointless, but
    nothing about infinity is involved.
\end{itemize}}

\paragraph{The ring: the staircase.} Four or more windows in a loop,
$\{x_0,x_1\},\{x_1,x_2\},\dots,\{x_{L-1},x_0\}$, each saying ``up''. With actual
numbers this is impossible: $x_0<x_1<\dots<x_0$, a Penrose staircase.
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \emph{The ideal number.} Let each window's answers follow one rule: the
    second slot behaves as if \emph{drawn later, from further out}. This is an
    ultrafilter, an ideal ``number at infinity''.
  \item \emph{Everything checks locally.} Every window genuinely believes
    ``up''. Neighbouring windows agree about every shared number.
  \item \emph{There is an answer pattern.} A consistent answer pattern exists for \emph{every}
    question built from the windows.
  \item \emph{But no reality.} No reality fits, not even an ideal one.
\end{itemize}
The whole is coherent and has no point. \emph{(Theorem~\ref{thm:witness}, Lean,
every $L\ge4$; Proposition~\ref{prop:stair}.)}
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}
\foreach \k/\a in {0/135,1/45,2/-45,3/-135} \coordinate (r\k) at (\a:1.2);
\draw[win] (r0)--(r1); \draw[win] (r1)--(r2); \draw[win] (r2)--(r3); \draw[win] (r3)--(r0);
\foreach \k in {0,...,3} \node[var] (q\k) at (r\k) {$x_\k$};
\draw[up] (q0)--(q1); \draw[up] (q1)--(q2); \draw[up] (q2)--(q3); \draw[up] (q3)--(q0);
\node[font=\scriptsize,align=left,anchor=west] at (2.0,0.35) {every window: ``up'' \ \checkmark};
\node[font=\scriptsize,align=left,anchor=west] at (2.0,-0.1) {neighbours agree \ \checkmark};
\node[font=\scriptsize,align=left,anchor=west,red!70!black] at (2.0,-0.55) {around the loop: no reality,\\ not even an ideal one};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

\bp{\textbf{Walking through the staircase} (four slots).
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \emph{Single-slot questions.} ``Is $x_0=5$?'' No, and likewise for every
    number. ``Is $x_0$ even?'' The rule $p$ decides, say yes. Both windows that
    show $x_0$ (that is, $\{x_3,x_0\}$ and $\{x_0,x_1\}$) give the \emph{same}
    answer, since in both $x_0$ is ``a number at infinity'' governed by $p$.
    That is what ``neighbours agree'' means.
  \item \emph{The window question.} ``Is $x_0<x_1$?'' Yes. Whatever far-out
    value the rule has for $x_0$, the later, further-out $x_1$ beats it. The
    same holds in every window, including ``is $x_3<x_0$?'', because in window
    $\{x_3,x_0\}$ it is $x_0$ that is drawn later.
  \item \emph{A compound question,} built from two neighbouring windows: ``either
    ($x_0<x_1$ and $x_1$ even) or ($x_1$ odd and $x_2>x_1$)''. The two options
    exclude each other. Each window answers its own part:
    \begin{itemize}[nosep]
      \item if $p$ says ``$x_1$ even'', the first part is yes and the second no;
      \item if $p$ says ``odd'', the reverse.
    \end{itemize}
    Either way, exactly one ``yes''. On a ring of four or more, every buildable
    question has this two-neighbouring-windows shape, and this is how each one
    gets answered (\S\ref{sec:forced}).
  \item \emph{The question that would break it.} ``Is $x_0<x_1<x_2$?'' would have
    to be answered yes, and then ``$x_0<x_1<x_2<x_3<x_0$'' would follow, which is
    impossible. But ``$x_0<x_1<x_2$'' is \emph{not a buildable question}: its
    slice at each value of $x_1$ is not a single-window cylinder
    (\S\ref{sec:shape}). As with the die, the contradiction lives only in
    questions that cannot be asked.
  \item \emph{Why there is no ideal reality.} An ideal reality \emph{does} answer
    every question about the four slots, including the joint one. It would have
    to say yes to all four ``up''s and so to their impossible intersection.
  \item \emph{Two copies, so you can keep them apart.}
    \begin{itemize}[nosep]
      \item \emph{Die:} pointless, finitely many questions.
      \item \emph{Staircase:} pointless, infinitely many questions, and every
        window's answers come from an ideal number.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}}

\paragraph{Demanding a real answer kills it.} Ask the staircase's answer pattern ``which
value is $x_0$?'', split into $x_0=0$, $x_0=1$, and so on. The pattern says ``no''
to each option, but ``yes'' to ``$x_0$ is some number''. That is allowed when only
\emph{finitely} many options need to add up. Now demand that the answers add up
across \emph{infinitely} many options ($\sigma$-additivity).
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item Each window's answers are then forced to be those of one definite pair of
    numbers.
  \item The staircase becomes impossible again.
\end{itemize}
Insisting that the answers be completed into a reality forbids the coherent
pattern.
\emph{(Proposition~\ref{prop:dirac}.)}
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[x=0.9cm]
\draw[thick,-{Stealth}] (-0.3,0)--(8.6,0) node[right,font=\scriptsize]{$\infty$};
\foreach \n in {0,...,7} {\draw (\n,0.08)--(\n,-0.08); \node[below,font=\scriptsize] at (\n,-0.08) {\n};
  \node[above,font=\scriptsize,red!70!black] at (\n,0.08) {no};}
\draw[decorate,decoration={brace,amplitude=4pt},thick] (0,0.55)--(8.4,0.55)
  node[midway,above=4pt,font=\scriptsize,blue!60!black]{``$x_0$ is some number'': yes};
\end{tikzpicture}\\[-2pt]
{\scriptsize The pattern's $x_0$ lives ``at infinity'': no to every value, yes to the whole line.
Demanding that the ``no''s add up to the ``yes'' ($\sigma$) is impossible.}
\end{center}

\bp{\textbf{Walking through the collapse.}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \emph{Finite splits are fine.} ``Is $x_0\le 100$?'' No. ``Is $x_0>100$?''
    Yes. One ``yes'' out of two, which is consistent. The same works for any
    finite split.
  \item \emph{The infinite split.} ``$x_0=0$'', ``$x_0=1$'', ``$x_0=2$'', \dots:
    every one is ``no''. The pieces cover everything, so $\sigma$-additivity
    demands that the ``yes''es among them add up to the whole's ``yes'', which is
    1. But they add to 0.
  \item \emph{So under $\sigma$} some ``$x_0=n$'' must get a yes. Then every slot
    gets an actual value, the windows describe one actual assignment, and four
    actual numbers cannot each beat the one before around a loop.
  \item \emph{The point.} $\sigma$-additivity is the demand that the answers be
    completed into a reality, and on this board that demand alone rules out the
    coherent pattern.
\end{itemize}}

\paragraph{The triangle: the open puzzle.} Three windows,
$\{x_0,x_1\},\{x_1,x_2\},\{x_2,x_0\}$, each touching both others.
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \emph{Loop-only questions.} There are now questions that cannot be
    broken into separate questions to individual windows. (On a ring of four or
    more, every question breaks into questions to at most two neighbouring
    windows.)
  \item \emph{With two values,} ``are all three equal?'' is one. It is the
    ``not'' of a question that \emph{does} break up, but it does not break up
    itself.
  \item \emph{With three values,} ``all equal'' is not even askable. There are
    2{,}088 questions such that neither they nor their ``not'' break up. The
    simplest is a nine-point cyclic pattern.
  \item \emph{Still open.} Can the staircase $x_0<x_1<x_2<x_0$ still have a
    consistent answer pattern and no reality? Evidence says yes: on a faithful finite
    part of the board, a consistent answer pattern exists. Nobody here has built one.
\end{itemize}
\emph{(\S\ref{sec:conj}; machine evidence only.)}
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}
\foreach \k/\a in {0/90,1/-30,2/210} \coordinate (t\k) at (\a:1.1);
\draw[win] (t0)--(t1); \draw[win] (t1)--(t2); \draw[win] (t2)--(t0);
\foreach \k in {0,1,2} \node[var] (u\k) at (t\k) {$x_\k$};
\draw[up] (u0)--(u1); \draw[up] (u1)--(u2); \draw[up] (u2)--(u0);
\node[font=\scriptsize,align=left,anchor=west] at (1.9,0.3) {every window touches both others};
\node[font=\scriptsize,align=left,anchor=west] at (1.9,-0.15) {Specker's three boxes; Penrose's tribar};
\node[font=\scriptsize,align=left,anchor=west,orange!80!black] at (1.9,-0.6) {answer pattern with no reality? \ open};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

\bp{\textbf{Walking through the triangle.}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \emph{What changes.} On the ring of four, windows $\{x_0,x_1\}$ and
    $\{x_2,x_3\}$ share nothing, and that forces every buildable question to
    live on two neighbouring windows. On the triangle every pair of windows
    shares a slot, so questions can braid all three.
  \item \emph{An example, with two possible values.} ``Are all three equal?'' is
    buildable. It is the opposite of a question that splits into one window
    question each, but it does not split up itself.
  \item \emph{With three values,} ``all equal?'' is not buildable at all. There
    are genuinely loop-only questions instead (2{,}088 of them).
  \item \emph{Why the ring's method fails.} The ring's rule answered each
    question from two windows. Loop-only questions need a rule that looks at the
    whole triangle at once.
  \item \emph{What is open.} Whether a consistent pattern still exists with every
    window saying ``up''. A computer check on a faithful finite part of the
    board finds one, but nobody has built it on the whole board.
\end{itemize}}

\paragraph{The moral.} In every example the contradiction lives only in a
question that cannot be asked: an intersection the board does not contain
(Lemma~\ref{lem:basic}). A loop is what lets every local view be right while the
whole is nothing. Infinity is what lets that survive. Demanding a completed
whole is what forbids it.

\section{Glossary}\label{sec:glossary}

Each entry gives the plain meaning, an example from the boards above, and the
exact object.

\begin{description}[style=nextline,leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=3pt]
\item[question; window question]
  \emph{Plain:} a yes/no question about the slots.
  \emph{Example:} ``is $x_0<x_1$?''
  \emph{Exact:} a window event, i.e.\ a set $\{x:(x_i,x_j)\in A\}$.
\item[buildable question; the carrier]
  \emph{Plain:} the questions you can make from window questions using ``not''
  and ``exactly one of these exclusive options''.
  \emph{Example:} ``either ($x_0<x_1$ and $x_1$ even) or ($x_1$ odd and
  $x_2>x_1$)''.
  \emph{Exact:} the carrier $\cD$, the Dynkin closure.
\item[unaskable question]
  \emph{Plain:} a combination of questions that is not itself buildable,
  typically an ``and''.
  \emph{Example:} ``is it in $\{4,5,6\}$ \emph{and} $\{6,1,2\}$?'' on the die;
  ``is $x_0<x_1<x_2$?'' on the ring.
  \emph{Exact:} an intersection not in $\cD$.
\item[consistent; coherent; state; answer pattern]
  \emph{Plain:} a yes/no answer to every buildable question such that, whenever
  a question is split into two exclusive parts, the ``yes''es add up right.
  \emph{Example:} ``no'' to all four die questions.
  \emph{Exact:} a two-valued finitely additive state on $\cD$.
\item[fits; gives; realises]
  \emph{Plain:} an assignment of values \emph{fits} an answer pattern if
  reading the answers off that assignment gives exactly the pattern.
  \emph{Example:} $1,2,3,4$ fits ``up'' on the path; no face fits ``no to all
  four''.
  \emph{Exact:} $s=\delta_\omega$, a point.
\item[ideal point; ideal reality]
  \emph{Plain:} a recipe that answers \emph{every} question about \emph{all} the
  slots at once, including every ``and'', consistently, without naming
  values.
  \emph{Example:} ``draw $x_0$ far out, then $x_1$ later, then $x_2$ \dots'' on
  the path.
  \emph{Exact:} an ultrafilter $W$ on $\Omega$. Equivalently, the yes-answers
  have the \emph{finite intersection property}: any finitely many of them
  share at least one possibility.
\item[pointless]
  \emph{Plain:} consistent, but fitted by no assignment and by no ideal recipe.
  \emph{Example:} the staircase on four slots.
  \emph{Exact:} neither a point nor an ideal point.
\item[extends; lifts]
  \emph{Plain:} answers given on part of the questions (one window, a few
  questions) are \emph{kept} while answers to all the other buildable questions
  are added consistently. Elsewhere in the programme ``lift'' names the same
  move.
  \emph{Example:} the window rules of the staircase extend to the whole ring
  (Lean), but not $\sigma$-additively.
  \emph{Related, the OML lane's $\sigma$-liftability ($\Phi$):} whenever finitely
  many answers come from \emph{some} consistent pattern, the same finitely many
  answers also come from some $\sigma$-additive pattern. The ring carrier fails
  this: ``all four `up's yes'' comes from the staircase and from no
  $\sigma$-additive pattern. (\texttt{oml\_attack/CAMPAIGN\_LOG.md}; Lean
  \texttt{phi\_iff\_dense}.)
  \emph{Exact:} a state on $\cD$ agreeing with the given one where it is
  defined.
\item[marginal; neighbours agree]
  \emph{Plain:} what a window's answers say about one of its slots alone.
  Neighbours \emph{agree} when both windows showing a slot answer every
  single-slot question the same way.
  \emph{Example:} both windows showing $x_0$ say ``$x_0$ even: yes''.
  \emph{Exact:} equal projections of the window ultrafilters to the shared
  coordinate.
\item[glued]
  \emph{Plain:} the windows are tied together only through the slots they share.
  \emph{Exact:} the carrier generated by the window events and nothing else.
\item[amalgamate]
  \emph{Plain:} combine two neighbouring windows' ideal recipes into one ideal
  recipe for all their slots, keeping both.
  \emph{Example:} $\{x_0,x_1\}$ and $\{x_1,x_2\}$ combine into ``draw $x_0$, then
  $x_1$, then $x_2$''.
  \emph{Exact:} an ultrafilter on the product projecting to both
  (Lemma~\ref{lem:amalg}).
\item[flat; non-flat]
  \emph{Plain:} the windows' recipes \emph{all} amalgamate into one recipe for
  everything (flat), or they cannot (non-flat).
  \emph{Example:} every path is flat; the staircase is non-flat.
  \emph{Exact:} Definition~\ref{def:flat} (tentative).
\item[$\sigma$-additive]
  \emph{Plain:} the ``yes''es must add up even when a question is split into
  infinitely many exclusive options.
  \emph{Example:} ``which value is $x_0$?'' then needs exactly one ``yes''.
  \emph{Exact:} additivity over countable disjoint unions.
\item[slice; cylinder]
  \emph{Plain:} \emph{slice}: fix one slot's value and look at what is left of a
  question. \emph{Cylinder}: a question that, after slicing, depends on only one
  remaining slot.
  \emph{Example:} at $x_1=7$, ``$x_0<x_1$'' slices to ``$x_0<7$'', a cylinder,
  while ``$x_0<x_1<x_2$'' slices to ``$x_0<7$ and $x_2>7$'', which is not.
  \emph{Exact:} sections of a set at a fixed coordinate value.
\end{description}

\section{Philosophical context}\label{sec:philo}

\bp{\textbf{The one question.} Is the whole \emph{given}, or \emph{set as a task}?
Each section below is a precise case of it. The philosophers here each name one
piece. Verification labels:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \st{read}: I read the passage itself;
  \item \st{bib}: confirmed through bibliographic records only;
  \item \st{sec}: confirmed through secondary sources only.
\end{itemize}}

\paragraph{Aristotle: the potential and the actual infinite.}
In \emph{Physics} III.6 (around 206a) the infinite ``is'' only potentially, in the
way a day or the games ``are'': there is always more to take, and the taking is
never completed. There is no actual infinite. \st{sec: Hardie--Gaye
translation and commentary; Bekker lines not checked against the Greek}.
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \emph{Here:} finite additivity is the potential infinite. It is coherent
    at every finite stage, with no completed sum demanded.
  \item $\sigma$-additivity is the actual infinite. The countable totality must
    be accounted for as completed.
  \item \S\S\ref{sec:dirac}--\ref{sec:stair}: taking the infinite as actual
    excludes a whole that the potential infinite allows.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Leibniz: space as an order, not a substance.}
Third paper to Clarke (1716, \S4 in the standard numbering): ``I hold [space] to
be an order of coexistences, as time is an order of successions.''
\st{read: English extract; section number from the standard numbering, not
checked}.
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \emph{Here:} points are outcomes of reconstruction from relations, not
    starting material.
  \item \S\ref{sec:paths}: along a path the reconstruction always succeeds.
  \item \S\ref{sec:stair}: around a cycle it can fail.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Kant: the totality as a task, not an object.}
In the Antinomy of Pure Reason, the series of conditions for an appearance is not
given as a whole. The regress is \emph{aufgegeben}. Section~7: ``daß ein
Regressus zu den Bedingungen \dots\ geboten oder aufgegeben sei''. Section~8 opens
with the \emph{regulative} principle: the totality is ``nicht \dots\ gegeben
\dots, sondern bloß im Regressus derselben aufgegeben'', valid ``nicht als Axiom,
die Totalität im Objekt als wirklich zu denken, sondern als ein Problem für den
Verstand''. Taking the totality as given (constitutively) is what produces the
antinomies. \st{read: German text, Gutenberg ed.; A/B pagination (\S8 opens
near A508/B536) from secondary sources}.
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \emph{Here:} the regulative/constitutive split is the scaffold/object
    split.
  \item The conservativity results say when treating the totality as given is
    harmless: $C=R$, finite alphabets, acyclic overlaps.
  \item \S\ref{sec:stair} is a small antinomy. Every window judges ``up'',
    coherently. Read constitutively, as a point or a $\sigma$-additive state, the
    totality contradicts them. Read as a task, a finitely additive state, it does
    not.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Husserl: the thing in adumbrations, as an Idea in the Kantian sense.}
A spatial thing is given only one-sidedly, in adumbrations, and so inadequately.
\emph{Ideas}~I \S\S142--143 resolve this by making the thing's adequate givenness
an ``Idea in the Kantian sense'', the ideal limit of a harmonious, open-ended
synthesis. \st{sec: Mariani, \emph{Phainomenon} 34 (2022); Metzler Lexikon
``ad\"aquat''; Husserliana III/1 not checked}.
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \emph{Here:} this is the source of the recorded phrase ``the object as
    the open horizon of coherent refinement''. Windows are adumbrations, and EA
    is harmonious synthesis.
  \item \S\ref{sec:stair} is a case the picture does not anticipate: the
    adumbrations cohere perfectly, and there is no thing, not even an ideal one.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Whitehead and pointless topology: points built from regions.}
Whitehead's method of extensive abstraction (\emph{An Enquiry Concerning the
Principles of Natural Knowledge}, 1919) defines a point as an abstractive class of
regions, so points are derived, not primitive. \st{sec: Whitehead Research
encyclopedia}. Its modern mathematics is locale theory: Johnstone, ``The point
of pointless topology'', \emph{Bull.\ AMS} 8 (1983), 41--53 \st{bib}.
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item Points of a locale are completely prime filters, cousins of the
    ultrafilters here.
  \item Non-trivial locales can have no points at all.
  \item \emph{Here:} pointless coherence is the measure-theoretic sibling of a
    pointless locale, and the bridge runs through Stone duality.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Specker: the cycle, from the start.}
Specker, ``Die Logik nicht gleichzeitig entscheidbarer Aussagen'', \emph{Dialectica}
14 (1960), 239--246, the seed of Kochen--Specker. It opens with a parable from
the school of prophets at Arba'ila. A seer sets three boxes before his daughter's
suitors. Each suitor must name two boxes that are both empty or both full. Every
time, one of the two holds a gem and the other is empty. It also places the
problem beside the scholastic \emph{Infuturabilien}: does God's omniscience
extend to what would have happened? \st{read: Seevinck's translation,
arXiv:1103.4537}.
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \emph{Here:} Specker's three boxes are a 3-cycle that is pairwise
    consistent and jointly impossible. That is \S\ref{sec:stair}'s staircase
    with three steps.
  \item Contextuality was born as a question about whether counterfactual
    totalities are determinate.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Penrose: the cohomology of the impossible.}
R.~Penrose, ``On the cohomology of impossible figures'', \emph{Structural
Topology} 17 (1991), 11--16; reprinted in \emph{Leonardo} 25 (1992)
\st{bib: via T.~Phillips, AMS Feature Column, Oct.\ 2014}. The impossible
tribar is explained by first cohomology, with locally coherent pieces and no
global object. The same mathematics later became the sheaf-theoretic account of
contextuality.
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \emph{Here:} \S\ref{sec:stair} is literally a Penrose staircase. Every
    step goes up.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The constructivist nerve.}
A non-principal ultrafilter cannot be shown to exist without some choice. So the
anti-Platonic object, a whole without points, is built from a Platonic
ingredient placed \emph{locally}. A Brouwerian would call the witness a fiction.
That leaves the deepest question here: can pointless coherence exist
constructively, or does refusing the completed whole cost you completed parts?

\bp{\textbf{Where the two minds meet.} The philosophy asks Kant's question. The
mathematics answers it by theorems that say exactly when treating the whole as
given is harmless:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{acyclic and finite:} always;
  \item \textbf{cyclic:} a coherent world can refuse to be a thing (Specker,
    Penrose, the flagship);
  \item \textbf{cyclic and infinite:} the demand for a completed thing excludes a
    coherent world (the countable witness).
\end{itemize}
The flagship (the cycle) and the $\sigma$ lane (infinity) are two halves of one
Kantian investigation, and they meet in \S\ref{sec:stair}.}
